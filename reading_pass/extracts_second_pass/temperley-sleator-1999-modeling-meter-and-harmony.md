## §3 — Coupling facts: what the paper assumes upstream, what it hands downstream, its stated scope

**Upstream — what the program is given.**

- **A note list, nothing more.** 11: *"The input we assume is a "note list," giving the pitch (in
  integer notation, middle C = 60) and the on-time and off-time (in milliseconds) of a series of
  notes."* And: *"The program requires no information beyond this: in particular, it does not require
  other information commonly available in scores, such as bar lines, key signatures, rhythmic
  notation, and the spellings of pitches."* 10 states the same in brief: *"As input, the program takes
  a list of notes with pitch, on-time, and off-time"*.
- **Unquantized timing is admitted.** 14: the input is *"first quantized into very short segments of 35
  msec, which we call pips"*; *"Every event onset and offset is rounded to the nearest pip; beats also
  may occur only at the start of a pip."* 19: the pieces tested include *"quantized files generated
  from scores (e.g., Finale files), and unquantized files generated from live performances on a MIDI
  keyboard."*
- **Polyphony is admitted.** 11 sets the program against earlier meter systems, *"all of these systems
  are limited to monophonic music. The current system attempts to overcome these limitations."* The
  length of a note in polyphony is handled by the registral inter-onset interval (§4.1).
- **Pitch enters unspelled.** The integer pitch carries no spelling; the program chooses one (§4.2,
  the pitch-variance rule).
- **What is stated NOT to enter.** Bar lines, key signatures, rhythmic notation and spellings (11, as
  quoted). Loudness, 25: *"It would be quite easy to incorporate loudness as a factor in our system;

  *"we have not yet addressed the major task of incorporating it."* Grouping structure, 25: *"getting a
  computer to recognize grouping boundaries proves to be a very difficult problem, and our preliminary
  efforts have been unsuccessful."* Key: the program labels roots *"in absolute terms"* (15), and 27
  names key-finding as other work (*"We are currently investigating some possible solutions to this

## §4 — The method, as the paper states it

### §4.1 Meter (11–15)

**The model it builds on.** 11: *"it is most closely related to the theory of Lerdahl and Jackendoff,
as presented in A Generative Theory of Tonal Music (hereafter called GTTM) (1983)."* A metrical
structure is *"several levels of equally spaced beats (where beats are points in time, not necessarily
events). Every second or third beat at one level is a beat at the next level up."*

**The three rules**, in the paper's wording (12, 14), which it says *"differs slightly from that in
GTTM"* (12):

- **Event rule** — *"prefer a structure that aligns beats with event onsets"*. 13: *"the more event
  onsets at a time point, the better a beat location it is."*
- **Length rule** — *"prefer a structure that aligns strong beats with onsets of longer events"*.
- **Regularity rule** — *"prefer beats at each level to be maximally evenly spaced"* (14). 13: *"The
  GTTM rules state that beats at the "tactus" level and immediately higher levels must be exactly evenly
  spaced"*; 14: the paper makes regularity a preference rule *(★ CORRECTED AT THE USER-ORDERED CHECK, §10.
  FORMER WORDING, PRESERVED (#12): "13–14: GTTM makes even spacing a well-formedness rule; the paper
  makes it a preference rule" — wider than p. 13, which names the tactus and immediately higher levels
  only)* so that the meter
  can follow tempo change: *"we simply make the requirement for regularity of beats at each level into
  a preference rule, rather than a well-formedness (hard-and-fast) rule."*

**Length of a note.** 13: inter-onset interval (IOI) *"works fairly well in monophonic music (although
even there it encounters problems, as we will show); however, it is totally unworkable in polyphonic
music."* The **registral IOI**: *"the inter-onset interval to the next event within a certain range of
pitch: we adopt the value of nine semitones."* With Figure 3: *"the length of a note is the maximum of
its duration and its registral IOI."* The paper calls the solution *"crude but fairly effective"* and
says separating a texture into lines is *"a highly complex task that we will not address here."*

**Quantization and meter finding as one process.** 14: *"an important recent realization of music
artificial intelligence has been that quantization and meter finding are really part of the same
process."* Rosenthal 1992 is named as a model that does both; *"Our system is similar in this respect,
performing both quantization and meter finding in a single process."*

**Scoring, for one level of beats** (14):

- The pip: 35 msec, *"(the value of 35 msec was simply found to be optimal through trial and error)"*.
- Tactus range considered: *"say 400–1,600 msec, the typical range for the tactus level."*
- **Note score**: each pip carrying a beat gets *"a numerical score indicating how many events have
  onsets at that point. This score is also weighted by the length of the events starting there."*
- **Beat-interval score**: *"the regularity of a beat Bn is simply given by the absolute difference
  between the interval between Bn and Bn–1 and that between Bn–1 and Bn–2."* It *"therefore acts as a
  penalty"*.
- **The square-root weight**: summing note scores would favour more beats, *"Thus, we weight the note
  score of each beat by the square root of its beat interval (the interval to the previous beat). The
  square root was chosen simply because it is a convenient function whose growth rate is between a
  constant (which would be too small) and a linear function (which would be too large)."*
- *"The preferred tactus level is the one whose total note score and (negative) beat-interval score is
  highest."*

**The other levels** (15). *"Five levels prove to be sufficient for the great majority of pieces. We
have adopted the convention of calling the tactus level 2, the upper levels 3 and 4, and the lower
levels 1 and 0."* Level 3 is generated as level 2 was, *"with the added stipulation that every beat at
level 3 must also be a beat at level 2, and exactly one or two level-2 beats must elapse between each
pair of level-3 beats."* Level 4 from level 3 the same way. *"There is no explicit requirement for
regularity in terms of the relationship between levels (duple or triple), but this tends to emerge
naturally"*. Level 1: a duple or triple division per level-2 beat, chosen by the same rules, *"there is
also a penalty for switching from duple to triple from one tactus beat to another."*

**A level-4 adjustment** (25): *"At level 4, the program ignores the length rule and simply prefers beat
locations that hit the maximum number of event onsets. In addition, we give a slight bonus at level 4
for placing a level-4 beat on the first level-3 beat of the piece"*. The paper calls this *"a cruder
solution"* in place of grouping structure, and says *"This fix improves performance somewhat, but
incorporating grouping structure would clearly be more satisfactory."*

### §4.2 Harmony (15–18)

**What is labeled.** 15: *"The program produces a representation of segments, which we call chord
spans, labeled with roots."* *"A possible harmonic analysis is simply a complete segmentation of the
piece, where each segment is labeled with a root."* On the answer being arguable, 15: *"(Unlike metrical
structure—which is usually indicated by the score of a piece—the correct harmonic structure for a
piece is to some extent a matter of opinion. While there are certainly cases where people disagree
about the correct analysis for a piece, there is a great deal of agreement as well.)"*

**The five rules**, in the paper's wording:

- **Compatibility rule** (16) — *"prefer roots that result in certain pitch-root relationships. The
  following relationships are preferred, in this order: 1, 5, 3, ♭3, ♭7, ♭5, ♭9, ornamental"*. An event
  with no chord-tone relationship to the root is *"ornamental"*. The worked case: F and A under a root
  of F give 1 and 3, under D give 5 and ♭3, so F is preferred (16).
- **Ornamental dissonance rule** (16) — *"in labeling events as ornamental, prefer events that are (1)
  closely followed by another event a half-step or whole-step away in pitch, and (2) metrically
  weak"*. The ground stated: passing tones, neighbor tones, suspensions and appoggiaturas *"all share a
  common characteristic: they are closely followed by another event a half-step or whole-step away in
  pitch. In addition, we have found that there is a preference for ornamental tones to be metrically
  weak, although sometimes they are metrically strong."*
- **Harmonic variance rule** (16) — *"prefer roots such that roots of nearby chord spans are close
  together on the line of fifths"*. The line of fifths (Figure 4) is *"similar to the circle of fifths,
  but extending infinitely in either direction"*.
- **Pitch-variance rule** (17) — *"prefer spellings for pitch events such that nearby events are close
  together on the line of fifths"*. Spellings are *"tonal pitch classes (TPCs), as opposed to the twelve
  neutral pitch classes of atonal theory"* (16); the paper holds that *"these spelling distinctions are
  an important aspect of tonal harmony, and must be represented."* The compatibility rule *"refers to
  tonal pitch classes, not neutral ones"* (17): A♭-C-E♭ takes root A♭, not G♯.
- **Strong-beat rule** (17) — *"prefer to start chord spans on strong beats"*. The stated effect: *"This
  has the added effect of avoiding very short chord spans; since strong beats are never very close
  together, if a chord span is very short, either that chord span or the following one must begin on
  a weak beat."*

**Implementation** (17–18).

- Segments at the lowest metrical level, boundaries only there (§3).
- Roots: *"Since the number of possible roots is infinite, we arbitrarily limit it to a range of 48
  roots on the line of fifths, and stipulate that the first root must be in the range from F♯ to D♭."*
- Spelling: *"(A spelling must also be chosen for each pitch event. The way this is handled
  computationally is rather complex, and is not discussed here.)"*
- Compatibility: *"we give the segment a positive score for each event (or part of an event) in the
  segment that has a chord-tone relationship with the root given by the analysis; more preferred
  relationships result in higher scores."*
- Ornamental tones: *"each event that is not a chord tone of the root receives a penalty, depending on
  how "good" it is as an ornamental tone, that is, how closely followed it is by the next event a whole
  step or half step away, and how metrically weak it is; better ornamental tones receive lower
  penalties."*
- Strong-beat: *"we assign a penalty to each segment whose root is different from the previous segment,
  depending on the strength of the beat at that point; weaker beats result in higher penalties."*
- Harmonic variance (18): *"For each segment, we take the mean position on the line of fifths of all
  previous roots in the piece, weighted for recency, so that more recent segments affect it more. We
  then look at the distance of the current segment's root from this "center of gravity."* A penalty is
  assigned on that distance. The weighting function is not stated on the pages as read.

**No numerical value for a rule's weight or internal parameter was met in the pages as read**, beyond the
pip (35 msec), the registral window (nine semitones), the tactus range (*"say 400–1,600 msec"*), the root
range (48 roots) and the first-root range (F♯ to D♭); the 2-point no-change penalty belongs to the
hypothetical example of §4.4 and not to the program. 20: *"The weight of each preference rule relative to the others must be
specified. Many rules also involve internal parameters: for example, in the compatibility rule, the
relative preference of different pitch-root relationships. We have simply adjusted these parameters on
a trial-and-error basis. After many tests and adjustments, we have found a set of values that seems to
produce generally good results. (The parameters can easily be adjusted; users of the program may wish
to experiment with this.)"*

### §4.3 Why the search is needed (18)

*"If only the compatibility rule and the ornamental dissonance rule were involved, finding the preferred
analysis would be easy, since the root for each segment could simply be chosen in isolation. What makes
it difficult are the variance and strong-beat rules. Because of these, the preferred root for a segment
depends on its context."* For meter, 15: *"the number of possible analyses increases exponentially with
the number of pips in the piece."*

### §4.4 The search procedure (18–19)

**Dynamic programming**, citing Cormen, Leiserson and Rivest 1990 (18). *"The procedures for the harmonic
and meter preference-rule systems are similar"*.

**The worked example** (18–19, Figure 5): twelve roots, five segments, only the compatibility rule and a
*"no-change" rule*, *"which simply penalizes all harmonic changes (similar to the strong-beat rule,
except disregarding meter)"*, at 2 points per change. Over the first two segments, *"all 144
possibilities"*; *"The best analysis is C-G, with a score of 5 + 6 – 2 = 9."* Then, per root at each
segment, the best-so-far score and the previous root it entails are kept; *"S3 only cares about what the
root was in the previous segment; anything prior to that is irrelevant."* The traced best path: *"In
this case, it is C-G-E-E-A."* **DERIVED, this reader's check at Figure 5:** the final column's highest
entry is A, *"6 (21,E)"*; back through E at segment 4 (*"5 (17,E)"*), E at segment 3 (*"5 (12,G)"*), G at
segment 2 (*"6 (9,C)"*), C at segment 1 (*"5"*) — C-G-E-E-A, total 21. ✓

**The garden-path effect** (19): *"at S3 the preferred root is G. But consider S4; here the preferred root
is E, and it points back to a root of E at S3. In this way, the system naturally handles the
"garden-path" effect: the phenomenon of revising one's initial interpretation of a segment based on what
follows."*

**Meter** (19): columns are pips, rows are beat intervals; a beat analysis steps only in certain columns;
*"There is a penalty for switching rows (i.e., beat intervals) from one step to the next."* The entry for
a pip and beat interval is filled by *"looking back to some previous pip (determined by the beat interval)
and choosing the best beat interval to use at that prior pip"*, where *"we have available the current beat
interval and the hypothetical previous beat interval, so we can assign the appropriate beat-interval
penalty."*

**Harmony** (19): *"The dynamic programming table at each segment only needs to be a one-dimensional
array"* for the compatibility, ornamental dissonance and strong-beat rules. *"To incorporate the harmonic
variance rule and the pitch-variance rule (determining the spellings of pitches), things get a lot more
complex, and we end up with a four-dimensional table (rather than a one-dimensional table) to fill in.
Space limitations prevent us from giving the details here."*

**Pruning** (19): *"In practice, the computation described above is intractable, because the
four-dimensional table gets too large. We handle this by pruning the table for a given segment immediately
after the table for that segment is constructed. We simply keep only those elements that are within some
constant of the highest score in the table. We have found a value for this constant that maintains a
reasonable speed for the system without compromising accuracy."* The constant's value is not stated, and
no measurement of the accuracy it does not compromise is shown on the pages as read.
