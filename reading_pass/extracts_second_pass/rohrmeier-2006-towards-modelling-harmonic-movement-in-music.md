## §3 — Coupling facts: what the study assumes upstream, what it hands downstream, its stated scope

**What it takes as input, and what that input does not carry.**

- **MIDI files**, four vocal parts. Printed 16: *"the available information for this study comprises
  key signature, metre (both Bach's original signatures), note onset and duration, given in beats."*
  **MIDI pitch carries no spelling**; the formalism reduces each pitch modulo 12 (printed 14, the projection τ), and
  no pc set this reader met in the appendices is printed with a flat — *"(C.D#.G)"* is the minor tonic triad,
  *"(C.E.G.A#)"* the dominant-seventh chord on the tonic. **So enharmonic equivalence is both chosen (printed 10: "chromatic
  (enharmonically equivalent) pitch classes") and forced by the data.** A visible consequence: the key
  alphabet has one symbol per pitch class, and Example 7.6 (PDF 106) labels a window inside an A-major
  chorale *"Ab"* and another *"eb"*.
- **No fermatas.** Printed 16: *"the representation particularly lacks of information on fermatas –
  therefore, aspects of phrasing could not be included into the research, and segmentation algorithms
  such as Eerola (2003) turned out to be relatively unreliable for the chorales."* **The study has no
  phrase boundaries.**
- **One metre per file**; chorales that change metre are named (*"B203⁵&204, B350"*, footnote 5:
  numbering by the Breitkopf edition).

**What the representation throws away, in the paper's words** (printed 10): *"A pc set representation
reduces a chord to an unordered set of chromatic (enharmonically equivalent) pitch classes,
disregarding absolute pitch and multiple repetitions of pitch classes. Hence, different arrangements or
inversions of the same pitch material will be regarded as the same pc set."* **So: no bass, no
inversion, no doubling, no voice, no register, no duration in the counts, no metric weight in the
counts.** Two consequences the paper itself shows:

- **The root is not recoverable from the alphabet.** The text writes *"II⁷ or IV⁶₅"* and *"VI⁷ or I⁶₅"*
  for single pc sets (printed 23), and Table 5.1 (PDF 88) leaves the scale-degree cell of *(C.D.F.A)*
  and *(C.E.G.A)* blank.
- **The paper chose this deliberately, against harmonic analysis.** Printed 9: harmonic analysis
  *"would nevertheless incorporate a rather large corpus of rule-based musical knowledge. But this is
  incongruent with the perspective of this study, which aims to connect bare statistical properties
  with cognitive aspects of basic musical structures. Therefore, a representation of vertical structure
  will be adopted which is closer to the musical surface and avoids taking up too much a priori music
  theoretical baggage."* It gives the standard difficulties as reasons: telling harmonic from
  non-harmonic notes; that *"the same chord G-C-E can have two interpretations as second inversion of
  the C major triad, or a 6-4 suspension of a G major triad in a dominantic context"*; incomplete
  chords; and *"it is far from clear whether a greater proportion of harmonic mappings of musical
  surface is not intrinsically ambivalent."*

**The one upstream judgment the frequency tables and the key induction rest on: the key of each
piece.** Sequences are counted *"relative to the key of the piece"* (printed 18) — each chorale is transposed so that its
**global** key is C major or C minor, and that global key is **assigned by the author's own rule-based
procedure** (§4.4 below), not taken from an annotation. The paper says why it matters (printed 21):
*"in order to do key inference based on pc set chains, the normalising relation of pc set chains to a
key has to be as reliable as possible"*; and again (printed 35): *"the potential of the method stands
and falls with the quality of the initial normalisation of the database"*.

**And the paper states what that normalisation does to the key profiles it then builds** (printed 34):
*"it is important to keep in mind that the key-profiles are built from the global key of the entire
pieces. Thus, the profiles also incorporate information in modulation structures – and thus rather
represent an overall distribution."* **In plain words, and by construction: the "C major" table contains every passage of
every major-key chorale, the passages that have modulated to the dominant or the relative minor among
them, all counted as C major.**

**What it hands downstream.** Frequency tables of pc sets and of pc-set n-grams (n = 1 to 4 printed; up
to 24 counted), per mode, under three segmentations; a key-induction method that looks a window of pc
sets up in those tables; and a sliding-window display of the keys so induced.

**Stated scope and stated limits.** One composer, one genre, 386 pieces. Printed 34: the n-gram profile
*"will expose restricted applicability in cases where the vertical structure is changed, such as
extended harmony or jazz chords. The key profiles need to be compiled from a corpus from the particular
style."* Printed 35: *"unknown pieces will relatively certainly contain n-grams which have not been
processed before"*. Printed 43, among future work: *"the key induction method could be extended to be
fully applicable for unknown pieces."* **So by the paper's own account the key-induction method, as
built, is not fully applicable to a piece outside the corpus it was counted from** *(★ narrowed at §10;
it read "is not applicable")*.

## §4 — The method, as the paper states it

### §4.1 Segmentation (printed 10–12; Figure 3.1, PDF 86)

Four ways of cutting the four-part texture into a sequence of pc sets are described; three are used.

1. *"'maximal' segmentation"* — *"vertical cuts of the piece at each change of the smallest common time
   unit"*. Described and set aside: it *"reduplicates held chords"*.
2. ***"dense segmentation"*** — *"segment only at those time positions where at least one voice/note
   event changes"*. Footnote 1: *"In Huron's HUMDRUM, a comparable processing method termed "full
   expansion" is implemented (Huron 1999)."* Its stated weakness: it *"treats all note events equally
   and there is no distinction between a semiquaver passing event and a crotchet chord."* Formally
   (printed 13): S = {k(oᵢ)}, k(t) = {i | oᵢ ≤ t ∧ oᵢ + dᵢ > t} — **the set of notes sounding at each
   onset, held notes included.** *(In this repository's vocabulary that is the constant-sonority
   slice, cut at onsets; the gloss is this reader's. Whether a release with no simultaneous onset also
   cuts is not settled by the formula, which indexes segments by onsets oᵢ only.)* Under this
   segmentation a repeated pc set is kept and means a re-voicing (printed 10).
3. ***"metrical segmentation"*** — the pc set sounding at each beat onset: S₂ = {k(t)} for integer t.
   Stated weakness (printed 11): *"it will include stressed dissonances or passing notes and not their
   resolutions. A quaver resolution of G-C-D into G-B-D will be treated as G-C-D."* The paper names
   Jørgensen & Madsen and Ponsford et al. as users of it.
4. ***"harmony approximation"*** (also *"harmonic approximation"*) — one pc set per crotchet beat,
   chosen among the pc sets occurring within that beat by rule R1, verbatim (printed 11): *"(R1) If the
   first chords of a set is dissonant, the least dissonant chord of the set will be preferred. If the
   first chord is consonant or a dominant seventh chord, it will be preferred."* Formally h(t) = arg
   max diss(k(j)) over the segments j in [t, t+1).

**The paper's own caveats on 3 and 4.** Printed 11: a V⁶₄ resolving to V⁵₃ at the quaver *"cannot be
easily and reliably distinguished from an instance of a I–V progression without reference to an
embedding context"*; examples 3.1 and 3.2 show passing formations the paper gives as cases
where *"problematic results will be produced"*. Printed 12: *"both
reductive segmentation methods share the assumption of a steady harmonic rhythm on a crotchet level.
This is already a rough-and-ready assumption with many exceptions"*.

**The dissonance score R1 uses** (printed 12; Table 3.1, PDF 87). Computed on the pc set's normal form
and interval vector (*"after Forte 1973"*), *"(non symmetry-invariant)"* — transposition classes, with
major and minor triads kept apart. *"The score results as the sum of the occurrences of each interval
multiplied by -4 for minor seconds, -1 for major seconds, -1 for tritones and 0 otherwise. The special
case of an augmented triad is given a score of three. Furthermore ... triads are assigned a value of 2
and dominant sevenths (with and without fifth) a value of 1."* Table 3.1 lists the ratings of the pc
set genera met, from 2 (major and minor triad) and 1 (dominant seventh with and without fifth), through
0 (single pitch class, third, fourth or fifth), −1 (minor seventh chord, diminished triad, among
others), −2 (diminished and half-diminished seventh), **−3 (augmented triad)**, −4 (major seventh
chord, among others) down to −9. **The interval weights (−4, −1, −1) and the special values
(2, 1 and −3) are set by hand; the pages as read give no derivation of them, no fit and no test of R1's
output against a harmonic analysis.**

### §4.2 The counting (printed 12–15)

The formalism follows Pearce & Wiggins (2004). A piece is a sequence of note events ⟨onset, pitch,
duration⟩; a segmentation maps it to a sequence of symbols from the pc-set alphabet ξ. Probabilities
are relative frequencies: equation (1), p(e) = c(e) / Σ c(e′) over all n-grams of the same length; and
equation (2), the conditional maximum-likelihood estimate p(eᵢ | context) = c(eᵢ | context) / Σₑ c(e |
context). **No smoothing**, and the paper says so (printed 14): *"Pearce & Wiggins 2004 note that this
method is rather simple and will become problematic in contexts of sparse data. However, it will serve
for a simple application which does not aim to produce sequences, but just analyses the corpus of
pieces because unknown contexts cannot occur in this case."*

An *interpretation* is a map from pc-set sequences to sequences over a second alphabet Υ of keys
(printed 15): *"each pc set symbol is mapped to a key symbol which characterises the entire sequence
until that pc set."*

### §4.3 Doublets (printed 17–18)

Duplicate chorales are found by representing each piece as its first pc set in Forte standard form plus
the sequence of bitwise exclusive-or differences between consecutive pc sets, transposed accordingly,
and searching for equal sequences; *"another heuristic marked pieces as virtual doublets if their first
30 pc sets had been identical. Altogether, 61 doublets were found"*. Pieces that are *"fairly similar,
but differ (though often just slightly) by several pc sets"* were **kept**. A pairwise comparison of
per-piece pc-set distributions (printed 32) found the same doublets, and *"very similar, but distinct
versions of a chorale yielded a pc set distribution similarity of around 80%."*

### §4.4 The key of each piece — the author's rule-based key finder (printed 18–21; Table 4.1)

**Why a key finder of his own.** Printed 19, footnote 7, verbatim: *"Retrospectively, Krumhansl's
algorithm classified 79.53%, Temperley's (2001) algorithm 20.47% of the chorales correctly."* *(The two
figures sum to 100.00; see §7.)* Longuet-Higgins/Steedman and Holtzmann *"could not be used here as they
are based on monophonic input"*.

**Modal chorales** are not set aside. Printed 19: *"for present purposes, it seems more appropriate to
refer each example to a related major or minor key, because separating the chorales by modes or finales
may fragment the data into rather sparse groups"*; and *"modal chorales are nevertheless not
reduced/subsumed into a major-minor system. They are transposed to C, but their idiosyncratic harmonic
features will be reflected in the statistical results."*

**The rules** (printed 20). (1) *"if the final chord is minor, it is assumed to be the tonic and the
algorithm run ended"*. Footnote 8: using the first chord *"turned out to yield too many exceptional
cases"*. (2) Otherwise the key signature, read from the MIDI file (*"It was assumed (and was double
checked with numerous samples) that the key signature information in the database was correct (only
one exception was found)"*), leaves five candidate tonics; intersecting them with what a major final
chord allows leaves at most two; four preference rules decide: *"that Mixolydian is unlikely to end on
its dominant (case d,G), that the case of e minor, given an empty key signature is very rare (case
e,a), that Ionian will not end on its dominant (G,C) and that Dorian does not end on its dominant
(a,d)"*. Footnote 9 allows that the Ionian/Mixolydian case *"may indeed be debatable for some
chorales"*. Two chorales with irregular key signatures *"have been classified manually."*

**Table 4.1** (printed 21; all stated for an empty key signature). Key rule 2, final chord major: final
chord C / D / E / F / G / A / B → assigned key C / d / a / – / G / a / e; **number of cases 190 / 30 /
24 / 0 / 11 / 114 / 1; number of exceptions 0 / 0 / 0 / (blank) / 0 / 5 / 0.**

**How good the key finder is, as far as the pages say:** *"These rules yield a key estimation which
turned out to be appropriate for most of the pieces, which renders the rules a reliable heuristic."*
**No accuracy figure, no reference the output was compared with, and no statement of who judged

### §4.5 Key induction from n-gram profiles (§6, printed 33–35)

**The criticism it starts from** (printed 33): pitch-class profile methods and Temperley's Bayesian
method *"embody significant shortcomings: straightforwardly tone-based key-statistics disregard
important pieces of information such as relationships between notes or other cues such as metrical
position/weight of single notes ... But more importantly, vertical co-occurrences are not taken into
account."*

**The method, in the paper's words** (printed 34): the n-gram tables of the normalised corpus *"make
n-gram profiles that are characteristic of C-major/minor. ... A certain pc set sequence, as well as a
concrete distribution of pc sets can be correlated to all 24 (transposed) versions of the key profile
in order to calculate the best matching key(s)."* Footnote 12: *"Practically, not the key profiles, but
the pc set sequence in question will be transposed."* **No formula for the matching score was met in
the pages as read.** *(This reader's reconstruction, from the footnote and the example outputs: the
window's n-gram is transposed twelve ways and its relative frequency looked up in the major and in the
minor n-gram table; the best of the 24 wins. That is a reconstruction and not the paper's statement.)*

**Two claims made for it, neither with a figure on the page:** that *"sequences like G-a-C, or C-G-C-G would be rather
equally assigned to C-major or G-major"* by scale-based methods while here *"a clear tendency towards C
can be found in both cases"*; that *"C-D-Eb-G, D-F-Ab-C or Eb-G-B-D already distinctively distinguish
c-minor"*.

**The sparsity problem, stated and left open** (printed 35): remedies named — backing off from n-grams
to (n−1)-grams, combining with pitch-class profiles, reducing the input — and then: *"The implementation
and fine-tuning of these possibilities, as well as a comparison with other key finding approaches, is
potential for future work."* And: *"the sparsity problems would not be solved by just enlarging the
database as due to the Zipfian distribution of the elements, the size of the database would have to
grow exponentially"*.

### §4.6 The sliding-window model (§7.1, printed 38–41; Examples 7.2–7.7, PDF 103–107)

For window lengths n = 1 to 4 (*"sequence lengths of the value 1 (single pc-sets) to 4 turned out to be
practicable"*), the key induced from the last n pc sets is written under the last pc set of the window.
Run on dense segmentation and on harmonic approximation. **Two thresholds**, both hand-set (printed 39):
*"a threshold has been introduced to rule out cases which occur only once and thus are not
representative of the dataset"* — shown as *"\*"* — and *"another threshold of minimal significant
difference has been introduced to mark cases as ambiguities where two or more keys are assigned with
fairly equal scores (more than 90%)"* — shown as *"?"*. Examples 7.4 to 7.7 also print, per position, a *"Key
expectation"* (headed *"Expectation:"* in 7.7) and *"Pc set expectations"* obtained from the maximum-likelihood continuation of equation
(2).

The model keeps **one key per window and no memory beyond the window**. Printed 41: *"the sliding
window approach would favour only one interpretation at each time"* (footnote 13 allows two where
scores are near-equal). Printed 42: *"an entirely global notion of key for a piece is not part of the
model's architecture – and, clearly, cannot be reproduced with it."* The paper contrasts this with
Temperley (2001), *"which computes an entire key analysis from the very beginning to the current time
point"* (printed 38–39), and with Jackendoff's (1992) parallel analyses (printed 41).
