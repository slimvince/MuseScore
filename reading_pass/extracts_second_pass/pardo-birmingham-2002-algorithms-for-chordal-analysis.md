## §4 — The method, as the paper states it

### §4.1 The two tasks, and the units (27–29)

27: *"We divide harmonic analysis into two tasks. Segmentation is the task of splitting the music into
appropriate chunks (segments) for analysis. As a default, we assume segmentation takes place in the
time dimension. Labeling is the task of giving each segment the proper quality and root pitch."*

28–29 *(★ corrected at §10; it read "28–29, and again at 35:", which put 35's different wording
under these quotation marks)*: *"Harmonic change can only occur when at least one note begins or ends. A
partition point occurs where the set of pitches currently sounding in the music changes by the onset
or offset of one or more notes. A segment is a contiguous interval between two partition points. A
minimal segment is the interval between two sequential partition points."* The definition names
**both the onset and the offset**. 35 restates it and names *"the ordered set (start to end) of all
partition points for a piece of music"* P_all, of size *p*.

30, on how minimal segments are built from MIDI: a twelve-element array indexed by pitch class holds
*"the count of notes of pitch class i currently sounding"*; *"When a non-zero MIDI delta-time is
encountered, this indicates the beginning of a new minimal segment, and the state is saved as a
minimal segment before updating it with the new note event."* **Read as written, a new minimal
segment begins at every non-zero delta-time**, which is the event-time form of the definition above;
the pages as read do not discuss an event that leaves the set of sounding pitch classes unchanged.

classes. Each class has twelve member templates, one for each root pitch class."*

### §4.3 Tie-breaking (30)

30: *"If two templates generate the same score, they form an equivalence class, and the winner may be
selected using any tie-resolution rule. Some tie-breaking rules we used with success in our system are
shown in Figure 4."*

**Figure 4 (30), as printed:** *"1. ROOT WEIGHT: Choose the template whose root pitch class has the
greatest weight of notes present in the segment. 2. PRIOR PROBABILITY: Choose the template with higher
prior probability of occurrence (see Table 2). 3. DIM7 RESOLUTION: If all top templates are fully
diminished 7th chords, select the template whose root is one half-step below the root of the top

In the example the C major and A minor templates tie at 6; rule 1 selects C major, *"since the pitch
weight for C in the segment is 4, while that for A is 0"*. 30: *"The first two tie-breaking rules in

### §4.4 Segmentation as a search (35–39)

**The size of the problem (36).** A segmentation is a subset of P_all holding at least its first and
last elements; it is written as a binary number of length *p* whose first and last bits are 1, so
there are 2^(p−2) segmentations. Partition points number at most twice the notes, so a piece whose
every onset and offset is distinct has 2^(2n−2); a single block chord has 2^(2−2) = 1. *"Bach's Sinfonia
No. 1, a 21-measure solo keyboard piece, has 341 partition points and thus 2^339, or roughly 10^102,
segmentations."*

**The assumption that makes it tractable (36).** *"any system that performs harmonic analysis must
make assumptions to reduce the size of the search problem for a typical piece of music. In particular,
the assumption that the task of labeling a single segment has limited context sensitivity greatly
reduces the complexity of the task by allowing the reuse of sub-solutions between segmentations."* And:
*"The framework we propose in this article limits context sensitivity in order to constrain the search
problem."* 36, under *Segmentation as Graph Search*: *"The score of a segmentation is given by the sum
of the scores of its component segments."* *"For the purpose of this analysis, we assume that
individual segments may be scored without reference to their context. The segment-labeling method
described in this article has this property."*

**What a segment's score is (37).** *"segments containing two or more chords tend to get low scores.
Segments containing notes belonging to a single chord tend to get high scores. The score for an
individual segment should correspond to how much sense it makes to select that segment as a unit of
analysis for finding a chord label. Thus, the score of the high-scoring label for a segment serves as a
reasonable score for that segment."* **The label score is reused as the segment score; the paper
argues it and measures the consequence at Table 5 (§5.3).**

**The graph (Figure 9, 37):** one vertex per partition point, sorted by partition-point number; an
edge from each vertex *i* to every vertex *j* with *i* < *j*; the reward of edge (*i*, *j*) is the score
of the segment from *i* to *j*. Figure 10 (38) shows two paths over Figure 2's measure: C maj 6 + G7 2
= 8, and C maj 6 + G7 2 + Cmaj 3 = 11.

**Relaxation search (37–38).** Named from Cormen et al. 1990, *"guaranteed to find the best path
through a topologically sorted DAG in O(E) steps"*. Edges number 0 + 1 + 2 + … + (p − 1) < p²/2; with
*p* at most twice the notes the paper states O(n²) steps *"(assuming a fixed-time segment scoring
function)"*. Figure 11 (38) prints the loop: for each vertex *v* in temporal order, for each *u* with
an edge ending at *v*, if score(*u*) + score(edge(*u*, *v*)) > score(*v*) then predecessor(*v*) = *u*
and score(*v*) is updated. For the Sinfonia: *"(341²)/2 or roughly 58,000 steps"*.

**Why a bounded look-back is not available (38–39).** Knuth and Plass (1981) solve line-breaking as a
graph search whose speed *"depends on the fact that the maximum allowed line length is known in
advance"*. 39: *"Unfortunately, one does not know the harmonic rhythm of a piece of music beforehand.
It may be that the entire piece consists of a single chord or that it breaks into a thousand chords.
… It is impossible to say that a chord can have a maximal length of, say, twenty segments. Thus, we
are forced to do a full search on the graph of the entire piece if we are to find the optimal
segmentation."*

**HarmAn (39–41).** Motivated by real-time use (*"such as an interactive jazz accompanist"*): a
heuristic *"for finding a good (although not necessarily the best) segmentation in linear time"*.
**Figure 13 (40), as printed:** *"1. Mark the initial vertex (partition point) S and final vertex E
2. While there exists at least one umarked vertex in the DAG 3. Select an unmarked vertex v from the
graph 4. Select u, the vertex whose only path to v has no intermediate vertices 5. Select w, the
vertex whose only path from v has no intermediate vertices 6. if score(edge(u,w)) < score(edge(u,v))
+ score(edge(v,w)) 7. mark v 8. else 9. remove from the graph v and all edges connected to v"*. So a
partition point is kept when splitting there scores strictly higher than not splitting, and removed
otherwise — **a tie removes the point.** 40: for real-time use *"the selection of a vertex (step 3 in
Figure 13) must be done in temporal order"*; three edges are considered per partition point, *"a
maximum of six segment-evaluations per note"*, hence O(n).

The rationale (40–41): *"it picks the best local, short-term choice at every turn to work on the music
in a start-to-finish way"*; *"music unfolds in time from start to finish. Composers generally create
structures that can be understood with limited backtracking to previously heard passages."* [THEORY as
offered: no source is cited for it on the pages as read.]

## §6 — The claims, labeled

**FACT — measured in this paper, on the corpus and measure stated.**

1. With the answer key's segmentation given, the labeling method with all three tie-breaking rules
   scores a mean 88.66% (Table 4; 88.65 at Table 5) of graded minimal segments over 45 KP excerpts,
   standard deviation 10.72; without tie-breaking 84.54%, standard deviation 13.00. Thirteen excerpts
   are labeled perfectly with all three rules, six with none.
2. Tie-breaking lowered the score on three excerpts (2, 16, 20).
3. With the segmentation found by search, the mean falls to 76.50% (Relaxation) and 75.81% (HarmAn);
   on the thirteen perfectly labeled excerpts, to 86.89% and 86.08%.
4. HarmAn's segmentations reach a median 98.7% (forward) and 98.2% (backward) of Relaxation's total
   score on 32 piano pieces, never below 95%; random order a median 90.5%, lowest 72.2%.

**FACT — of the construction, established by the paper's argument and not by measurement.** Relaxation
