- **[FACT, p. 1 Abstract]** The paper's own summary of its gap: *"Although automatic chord recognition

  it impractical."*
- **[FACT, p. 1 Abstract]** What the paper says it presents: *"a comparison of several modelling
  strategies for chord recognition, hidden Markov models (HMMs) and conditional random fields (CRFs), on

  previous work, our models use pitch class profile (PCP) vectors for audio modelling."*
- **[FACT, p. 1 Abstract]** The headline claim: *"Our results show improvement over previous literature,

  several avenues for future work."* What the pages print in support of *"precise"* is at §5 below.
- **[FACT, p. 1 §1]** The departure point is Sheh and Ellis [13], who *"used HMMs to perform chord

  foundation for future study."*
- **[FACT, p. 1 §1]** The protocol: *"We use the same data set, but in addition to their tests, we
  perform a 10-fold cross validation to verify the validity of our results, training on 18 songs for each

- **[THEORY, p. 1 §1, citing [5] Hastie, Tibshirani & Friedman]** *"Cross validation is essential for

- **[CONJECTURE, p. 1 §1]** *"Cross-validated recognition rates will be lower than the best possible test

- **[FACT, p. 1 §1]** The flat start, defined: *"When the training data is not aligned, the model must be

### §2.2 The audio features and the two emission models (§2, pages 1–2; Figure 1, page 3)

- **[FACT, p. 1 §2]** Pitch class profile vectors *"were introduced by Fujishima in 1999 [3]"* and
  *"represent a logarithmically warped and wrapped version of the short time frequency spectrum."*
- **[FACT, p. 1 §2, equations (1) and (2)]** As printed: PCP[i] ≜ Σ over {k : p[k] = i} of ‖X[k]‖² (1);
  p[k] ≜ { round[ D log₂( (k/N) · (f_s / f_ref) ) ] } mod D (2). *"k is an FFT bin, f_ref is the
  reference pitch, N is the FFT window size, f_s is the sampling rate, and D is the dimensionality of the
  PCP vector."* The log₂ operation *"warps"* and the modulo operation *"wraps"* the spectrum *"at integer
  multiples (octaves) of the reference frequency"*; the components in each band *"are then summed"*.
- **[FACT, pp. 1–2 §2]** *"In this study, we used D = 12 and f_ref = 261.6 Hz (C4). Thus, each PCP vector
  represents 12 semi-tones of a chromatic scale under the same modulus as most Western music theorists as
  well as the MIDI note number specification."*
- **[FACT, p. 2 §2.1]** *"The choice of parameters used to model a PCP vector is not trivial."* Each
  dimension of *"the normalised PCP vector"* is continuous and *"can be modelled as a probability
  distribution."* Sheh and Ellis [13] *"modelled PCP vectors using single Gaussians."*
- **[FACT, p. 2 §2.1; Figure 1, p. 3]** *"Using our labelled training data, it is possible to show that
  single Gaussians do not provide an adequate model of the PCP vectors. Figure 1 shows 3 examples of a
  single Gaussian superimposed on 1 dimension of an A-minor PCP vector. Clearly a mixture of Gaussians
  would be more suitable to accurately model the shape of these distributions."* Figure 1's caption:
  *"Single Gaussian (smooth curve) plotted over three different dimensions of a PCP vector (histogram)."*
  **The text says *"1 dimension"* and the caption says *"three different dimensions"*;** as rendered, the
  second panel's horizontal axis ends at 0.5 and the third's at 0.25, and the first panel's tick labels are
  not legible at this rendering *(★ bounded at the user-ordered check, §10; former wording, preserved
  (#12): "the three panels have three different horizontal ranges as rendered" — two of the three ranges
  could be read)*. The evidence offered is the three pictures; no
  goodness-of-fit number is printed.
- **[FACT, p. 2 §2.1]** *"In this paper, we used a mixture of Gaussians to model the probability
  distributions of the PCP vectors, and as can be seen from the results, the models trained using a
  mixture of Gaussians outperform those using single Gaussians."* Table 2 gives more than one Gaussian
  count for the hidden Markov models and for no CRF; how far the sentence holds over those rows, and the
  one cell that goes against it, is at §4.2 below.
- **[THEORY, p. 2 §2.2]** *"Because all values in a normalised PCP vector must be non-negative and sum to
  one, one may think of them as the parameters to a hypothetical multinomial distribution."* The
  Dirichlet distribution is *"the so-called conjugate prior of the multinomial distribution, i.e., given
  a set of parameters that represent an archetypal multinomial distribution, it represents the
  probability that any other multinomial distribution might arise instead."*
- **[FACT, p. 2 §2.2, equation (3)]** *"Unlike mixtures of Gaussians, Dirichlets enforce the constraint
  that the output distributions be valid multinomial distributions, which is equivalent to the
  constraints on valid normalised PCP vectors. Moreover, they require fewer parameters to train"*. As
  printed: Dir(p, u) ≜ (1/Z(u)) ∏ from i = 1 to D of p_i^(u_i − 1) (3). *"The p_i correspond to the bins
  of the multinomial distribution, which are the 12 values of the PCP vector in our case. The parameter
  vector u = {u₁, u₂, …, u_D}, where D is the number of bins in the multinomial, determines both the mean
  and the variance of the distribution."* The normalisation term Z(u) *"is beyond the scope of this
  paper"*; the reader is sent to [2], *"an earlier application of Dirichlet distributions to chord
  recognition."*

### §2.3 The sequence models (§3, pages 2–3)

- **[FACT, p. 2 §3.1]** *"HMMs are generative stochastic models that attempt to model a hidden
  first-order Markov process based on a set of observable outputs."*
- **[CONJECTURE, p. 2 §3.1]** *"Nonetheless, it should be noted that in reality chord progressions are
  high-order Markov processes, and so the first-order Markov assumption may result in model
  deficiencies."* — asserted with no citation and no measurement on the page; no higher-order model
  is among the models §4 describes.
- **[FACT, p. 2 §3.1]** The model-discriminant (MD) approach, attributed to Sheh and Ellis [13]: *"every
  potential chord is modelled by its own left-right, single-state HMM. Each model is trained either
  individually or by using an embedded version of the Baum-Welch algorithm that concatenates the HMMs. In
  order to perform this step, one needs labelled—but not necessarily aligned—training data."* Recognition
  uses *"the Viterbi algorithm on the network of component HMMs to yield the most likely sequence of
  component models."* (The word *"models"* is set in italics on the page.)
- **[FACT, p. 2 §3.1]** The path-discriminant (PD) approach, attributed to Bello and Pickens [1] and Lee
  and Slaney [6]: *"every chord is modelled by one state in a larger, fully connected HMM, which can be
  trained with the expectation-maximisation (EM) algorithm and does not require labelled data."*
  Recognition uses *"the standard Viterbi algorithm to obtain the most likely sequence of states."* (The
  word *"states"* is set in italics on the page.)
  *(★ ADDED 2026-09-19 at the cross-check, §9.5. The page's text here reads *"Lee and Slaney [6]"*; in the
  reference list [6] is K. Lee alone and Lee and Slaney is [7]. The attribution above is carried as the
  page prints it.)*
- **[THEORY, p. 2 §3.2]** The paper's argument for conditional models: hidden Markov models *"seek to
  maximise the joint probability P(X, Y)"*; *"During recognition, the observation sequence is always
  fixed, and so it may make more sense to model only the conditional probability distribution P(Y|X).
  This frees the recogniser from needing to enforce any particular model P(X) of the data, and thus it is
  no longer necessary for the components of the observation vectors to be conditionally independent, as
  they must be for HMMs. Such a model may include thousands or even millions of observation features."*
- **[FACT, p. 2 §3.2, citing [14]]** The linear-chain CRF is *"one of the most commonly used member of
  the larger CRF family [14]"*; CRFs *"differ from the HMMs in that each hidden state depends not just on
  the current observation but on the complete observation sequence. At decoding time, linear-chain CRFs
  are quite similar to HMMs, using a variant of the Viterbi algorithm."*
- **[FACT, p. 2 §3.2]** The training requirement: *"Unlike an HMM, however, in order to train a
  linear-chain CRF, one must have access to fully labelled and aligned training data."*
- **[FACT, pp. 2–3 §3.2, citing [12]]** *"Training is considerably slower for CRFs than it is for an HMMs
  regardless of whether one uses a path-discriminant or model-discriminant approach, but fortunately,
  there are optimisation techniques to improve the training speed. We chose the limited-memory
  Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno method, a variant of Newton's method [12]."* (*"an HMMs"* is the
  page's.) The measured training times are at §4.4 below.

### §2.7 Summary and future work (§5, page 4)

- **[FACT, p. 4 §5]** *"Overall, our results compare favourably with previous research for this task, but
  they also suggest that on their own, PCP vectors may not be sufficient for reliable chord
  recognition."*

## §6 — Terms the paper uses, in the paper's sense

- **Pitch class profile (PCP) vector:** a 12-dimensional, logarithmically warped and octave-wrapped
  summary of one short-time spectrum frame, equations (1) and (2) (p. 1 §2); *"normalised"* PCP vectors
  are non-negative and sum to one (p. 2 §2.2).
- **Rotated / unrotated:** PCP vectors (and labels) transposed to C major using the song's known key,
  against PCP vectors and labels left as they are (p. 3 §4).
- **Flat start:** initialising a model by segmenting training audio uniformly from an unaligned
  transcription (p. 1 §1). (The paper's term; it is not the accidental.)
- **Model-discriminant (MD):** one single-state left-right HMM per chord, recognised as the most likely
  sequence of component models (p. 2 §3.1). **Path-discriminant (PD):** one state per chord in one fully
  connected HMM, recognised as the most likely sequence of states (p. 2 §3.1).
- **CRF-G, CRF-D, CRF-GD / CRF-DG:** linear-chain CRFs whose emission features are *"equivalent to a
  single Gaussian"*, *"equivalent to a Dirichlet distribution"*, and both together (p. 3 §4).
- **Gau.:** Table 2's column head for the number of Gaussians per PCP bin (MD; p. 3 §4) or per state (PD;
  p. 3 §4); the CRF-D row prints a dash.
- **Recognition rate:** correct frames divided by total frames, as a percentage, with no tolerance at
  chord boundaries (pp. 3–4 §4).
