## §2 — The method, as the paper states it

### §2.1 The decision rule and the model (§2, PDF pages 1–2)

- **Eq. 1:** ê(f) = argmax over e of {Pr(e|f)}. The argmax is named *the search problem*.
- The paper states that the Eq. 1 decision *"minimizes the number of decision errors"*, is optimal
  under a zero-one loss (Duda and Hart, 1973), and that *"using a different loss function—for example,
  one induced by the BLEU metric—a different decision rule would be optimal."*
- **Eqs. 2–3:** Pr(e|f) = p_λ(e|f) = exp[Σ_m λ_m h_m(e,f)] / Σ_{e′} exp[Σ_m λ_m h_m(e′,f)], with M feature
  functions h_m(e,f) and M model parameters λ_m.
- The *modeling problem* is developing feature functions; the *training problem* is obtaining λ.
- **Eq. 4, the MMI criterion** (maximum mutual information, *"which can be derived from the maximum
  entropy principle"*): λ̂ = argmax over λ of {Σ_{s=1..S} log p_λ(e_s|f_s)}.
- The paper states of Eq. 4: *"there is one unique global optimum, and there are algorithms (e.g.
  gradient descent) that are guaranteed to converge to the global optimum"*; and *"there is no reason to
  assume that an optimization of the model parameters using Eq. 4 yields parameters that are optimal
  with respect to translation quality"* (italics on *are optimal* in the print).

### §2.2 The evaluation criteria used (§3, PDF pages 2–3)

- **mWER**, multi-reference word error rate: edit distance (*"minimum number of substitutions,
  insertions, deletions"*) to the closest of the given reference translations.
- **mPER**, multi-reference position-independent error rate: the same, ignoring word order, the sentence
  treated as a bag of words.
- **BLEU**: geometric mean of n-gram precisions times a brevity factor BP(·); BLEU = BP(·)·exp(Σ_{n=1..N}
  log p_n / N); **N = 4**.
- **NIST**: a weighted n-gram precision times a factor BP′(·); NIST = BP′(·)·Σ_{n=1..N} w_n; **N = 4**.
- The paper states that NIST and BLEU are accuracy measures (larger is better) and that they *"are not
  additive for different sentences, i.e. the score for a document cannot be obtained by simply summing
  over scores for individual sentences."*

### §2.3 The training criterion for error rate (§4, PDF page 3)

- An error function E(r,e) compares a sentence e with a reference r; the paper says the exposition
  *"can be easily adapted to accuracy metrics and to metrics that make use of multiple references."*
- **Additivity is assumed:** E(r_1^S, e_1^S) = Σ_s E(r_s, e_s).
- Given a representative corpus f_1^S with references and, per sentence, a set C_s = {e_{s,1},…,e_{s,K}}
  of K candidate translations:
  - **Eq. 5:** λ̂ = argmin over λ of {Σ_s E(r_s, ê(f_s; λ))} = argmin over λ of {Σ_s Σ_k E(r_s, e_{s,k})
    δ(ê(f_s; λ), e_{s,k})}.
  - **Eq. 6:** ê(f_s; λ) = argmax over e ∈ C_s of {Σ_m λ_m h_m(e|f_s)}. *(The print writes h_m(e|f_s)
    here and h_m(e,f) in Eq. 3; transcribed as printed.)*
- **Two stated difficulties:** the argmax makes a gradient impossible to compute, so gradient descent
  cannot be used; and the objective *"has many different local optima"*. **A third, stated separately:**
  *"even if we manage to solve the optimization problem, we might face the problem of overfitting the
  training data."*
- **Eq. 7, the smoothed error count:** λ̂ = argmin over λ of {Σ_{s,k} E(e_{s,k}) · p(e_{s,k}|f)^α /
  Σ_k p(e_{s,k}|f)^α}, with α adjusting smoothness. *(The print writes E(e_{s,k}) without the reference
  argument, and f without the sentence index; transcribed as printed.)*
- For α → ∞, Eq. 7 *"converges to the unsmoothed criterion of Eq. 5 (except in the case of ties)"*. The
  smoothed objective *"might still have local optima"*, making optimization *"hard compared to"* Eq. 4.
  The smoothed error count is *"a common approach in the speech community (Juang et al., 1995; Schlüter
  and Ney, 2001)."*

### §2.5 The optimization algorithm (§5, PDF pages 3–5)

- **The standard algorithm the paper replaces:** *"Powells algorithm combined with a grid-based line
  optimization method (Press et al., 2002)"* (printed without the apostrophe); start at a random point,
  line minimization along directions given by optimizing one parameter with the others fixed; start from
  different initial values to avoid a poor local optimum. Its stated problem: a fine grid is slow, a
  coarse grid may miss the optimum. **No count of initial values and no grid size is printed.**
- **The new algorithm, for one line** λ + γ·d with parameter γ: each candidate's score along the line has
  the form t(e,f) + γ·m(e,f), with t and m constant in γ, so *"every candidate translation in C
  corresponds to a line."*
  - **Eq. 8:** ê(f; γ) = argmin over e ∈ C of {t(e,f) + γ·m(e,f)}.
  - **Eq. 9:** f(γ; f) = min over e ∈ C of {t(e,f) + γ·m(e,f)}, which *"is piecewise linear (Papineni,
    1999)"*.
  - *(Eqs. 8 and 9 print argmin and min; Eq. 6 prints argmax. The paper does not reconcile the
    direction. Recorded in §7.1.)*
  - Per sentence, the ordered interval boundaries γ_1 < … < γ_{N_f} and the change in error count at each
    boundary ΔE_1,…,ΔE_{N_f} are computed, ΔE_n being the change from the error count at position
    (γ_{n−1}+γ_n)/2 to that at (γ_n+γ_{n+1})/2. All sentences' boundaries and changes are merged; the optimal
    γ is found by traversing the merged boundaries while updating an error count.
  - Stated properties: *"guaranteed to find the optimal solution"* and *"much faster and more stable than
    the grid-based line optimization method."* **No timing, no stability measurement and no comparison
    against the grid method is printed anywhere in the paper as read.**
  - Extension to BLEU and NIST: *"straightforward"*, by *"accumulating the relevant statistics for
    computing these scores (n-gram precision, translation length and reference length)"*.
- **What §5 does not state:** how the line directions d are chosen in the new algorithm, whether random
  restarts are kept, how many lines or iterations are run, and any stopping rule for the outer search.

### §2.6 The translation system and the n-best procedure (§6, PDF page 5)

- Features *"identical to the alignment template approach (Och and Ney, 2002)"*, plus a phrase penalty
  (the number of alignment templates used) and *"special alignment features"*; **M = 8** features in all.
- *"many of the used feature functions are derived from probabilistic models: the feature function is
  defined as the negative logarithm of the corresponding probabilistic model."*
- Search: dynamic programming beam search (Och et al., 1999); n-best lists by A* search (Ueffing et al.,
  2002).
- **The n-best iteration, as printed:** first, search with *"a manually defined set of parameter
  values"* and compute an n-best list, and train on it; second, search with the new parameters and
  merge the new n-best list with the existing one; third, retrain on the extended list; *"iterated until
  the resulting n-best list does not change."* Convergence is *"guaranteed as, in the limit, the n-best
  list will contain all possible translations."* About **200** alternative translations per iteration;
  convergence after *"about five to seven iterations"*; *"As a result, error rate cannot increase on the
  training corpus."*
- **The MMI baseline's pseudo-references:** because the search prunes, the true references are often not
  in the n-best list; for MMI training, *"new pseudo-references"* are the n-best sentences *"which have a
  minimal number of word errors with respect to any of the true references"*. The paper states that MMI
  results *"might be biased toward the mWER criterion"* for this reason, and that not needing
  pseudo-references is *"a major advantage of the minimum error rate training"*.

### §2.7 The experimental setting (§7, PDF pages 5–6)

- The 2002 TIDES Chinese–English small data track; news text, Chinese into English. No rule-based
  components for numbers, dates or names. Feature functions trained on the training corpus; **the
  development corpus used to optimize the log-linear parameters**; results reported on the test corpus.
- Italic numbers: difference to the best (bold) result *"is not statistically significant"*.
- Confidence intervals: *"For all error rates, we show the maximal occurring 95% confidence interval in
  any of the experiments for that column"*, computed by bootstrap resampling (Press et al., 2002). **The
  sentence says "error rates"; the tables print intervals in the BLEU and NIST columns too.**
- The last column is the number of words in the produced translations, to be compared with the average
  number of reference words in Table 1.

## §3 — Coupling facts

### §3.1 What it ASSUMES about its upstream

- **A fixed log-linear model whose M feature functions are already trained** (§6: *"The basic feature
  functions were trained using the training corpus"*, §7). The method fits only the M combination
  weights λ.
- **A candidate set per input sentence** C_s, produced by a search (n-best by A*), and a **search that can
  be re-run** with new weights for the n-best iteration.
- **Reference translations** for the development corpus, several per sentence for the multi-reference
  criteria.
- **An error criterion that is additive over sentences** for Eq. 5 as written; for BLEU and NIST, which
  are not additive, sufficient statistics that are (§5's last paragraph).
- **A development corpus distinct from the test corpus** (§7).

### §3.2 What it HANDS downstream

- A weight vector λ (M = 8 values here), used by the unchanged decision rule of Eq. 6 inside the search.

  training is printed in the pages as read. The paper's own §2 frames the log-linear model as an approximation of Pr(e|f);
  **what minimum error rate training does to that reading is not discussed.**

### §3.3 Its own STATED scope and limits, in the paper's own words

- *"How many parameters can be reliably estimated using unsmoothed minimum error rate criteria using a
