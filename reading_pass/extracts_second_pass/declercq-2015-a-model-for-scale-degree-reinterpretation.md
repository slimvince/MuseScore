### §2.1 The problem the paper sets (pages 1–4)

1. **THEORY (Gauldin 2009, as the paper reports him).** Graduate students harmonizing a hymn melody in
   the style of Bach *"often have a kind of "tunnel vision" that causes them to interpret melodic
   fragments within only a single tonal center"* (page 1), so their harmonizations *"tend to lack the
   tonal variety of a Bach setting"*. Gauldin's remedy is *"scale-degree reinterpretation"*, which the
   paper glosses as *"the process of reinterpreting the notes of the hymn melody as diatonic scale
   degrees in some key other than tonic"* (page 2). Figure 3 (page 2, *"adapted from Gauldin 2009"*)
   sets the melody notes D-D-C four ways: in F as 6-6-5 (IV–IV6–I), in C as 2-2-1 (ii6/5–V–I), in A
   minor as 4-4-3 (iv6–V7–i), in B-flat as 3-3-2 (I6–IV7–V).
2. **CONJECTURE (the paper's own framing).** Reinterpretation *"does come at a cost"*: the single-key
   approach leaves, for a phrase ending on scale-degree 5, *"only three cadential possibilities"* (half,
   plagal or imperfect authentic, all in tonic; page 2), where reinterpretation gives *"at least ten
   cadence possibilities"* for the same phrase ending — Table 1, §4.1 below. The paper's questions
   (pages 2–3): *"How well does a particular melodic structure predict the cadence and local key area
   within the chorale harmonizations of Bach? When is scale-degree reinterpretation stylistic, and when
   is it not? Are certain types of scale-degree reinterpretation more typical than others?"*
3. **THEORY (prior counts).** McHose (1947) and Boyd (1967/1999) gave cadence statistics for the
   chorales; the paper reproduces Boyd's as Table 2 (§4.2 below) and says of it that *"it is not clear
   from Boyd's table (or his accompanying text) why or in what context (melodic or otherwise) to use
   each cadence type"* (page 3). Gauldin's textbook (1988/1995, p. 44) gives *"typical cadential
   formulas"* as outer-voice pairs, reproduced as Figure 4 (page 4): A. 7-8 over vii°6–I; B. perfect
   authentic, 2-1 and 7-8 over V–I; C. imperfect authentic, 2-3 and 5-3 over V–I; D. half, 3-2 over I–V
   and 8-7 over I6–V, and a Phrygian 4-5 over iv6–V in F minor; E. deceptive, 2-1 and 7-8 over V–vi.
   The paper's remark on them: *"none of the cadences in Figure 4 leave the tonic key"* (page 3).
4. **THEORY (the corpus-study and automated-harmonization literature, page 4), surveyed by the paper**
   *(★ CORRECTED at the user-ordered check, §10. FORMER WORDING, PRESERVED (#12): "named by the paper and
   not used by it" and, below, "The paper's stated reason for not building on the automated work" — a
   negative and a motive the page does not state)*: Rohrmeier and Cross (2008); Tymoczko (2011); Quinn (2010) on key-finding; Quinn and
   Mavromatis (2011); constraint-based harmonization (Ebcioğlu 1988, 1990); neural networks (Hörnel and
   Menzel 1998); Markov chains (Thorpe 1998; Biyikoglu 2003; Allan and Williams 2005). The paper's
   judgment of the automated work is that it has *"limited benefits for music theory pedagogy"*: *"even when these models are successful, it is
   difficult to infer any practical advice to a music student since a wide variety of parameters and
   settings are involved."*
5. **FACT (the study's stated design, page 4).** *"In the corpus study reported here, the parameters
   are purposefully limited so as to increase the pedagogical applicability. Specifically, this study
   investigates what kinds of modulatory and cadential events are typical at phrase endings given the
   various melodic structures of the 371 chorale harmonizations by J. S. Bach."*

### §2.3 Where a phrase ends and where its cadence arrives (pages 5–6)

10. **FACT (the rule used).** *"As a rule, fermatas were taken to delineate phrases but not necessarily
    to indicate the exact location of the cadential arrival."* And (page 6): *"cadence locations were
    determined on a case-by-case basis, with the cadential arrival (if any) taken as the last change of
    harmony at or before the fermata."*
11. **FACT (what the author observed, stated without counts).** *"Most commonly, the final chord of the
    cadence occurs under the fermata on beat 3 (as in chorale 323)."* Exceptions come *"when the
    fermata appears on a weak beat (i.e., beats 2 and 4 in 4/4; beats 2 and 3 in 3/4)"*. There, *"Bach
    exhibits a clear tendency to shift the cadential arrival to the strong beat when possible"*: where
    the weak-beat fermata is preceded by a unison (chorale 95) or a fall of a third (chorale 128), the
    arrival *"will normally occur on the strong beat prior to the fermata"*, the paper's reason being
    that *"the intervals of a unison and third can be contained within the same harmony"*; where it is
    preceded by a step (chorale 64), the arrival is *"displaced forward from its typical strong-beat
    location to coincide with the weak-beat fermata"*. Figure 5 (page 5) shows the four cases with an
    asterisk at each arrival. The paper generalizes: *"the preference for cadences on strong beats can
    be considered a general feature of the chorales, whether in 4/4 or 3/4 meter."* **No count of how
    often each case occurs is given on the pages as read.**

### §2.4 The melodic encoding (page 6)

12. **FACT.** *"the scale-degree content of the melody was encoded in terms of the global tonic for the
    last three beats of the cadence."* A half note is encoded as two instances of its degree (Figure 6,
    chorale 323: 6-6-5, 6-6-5, 2-2-1 over its first six bars).
13. **FACT.** *"In some cases, non-harmonic tones had to be stripped away, which required some
    analytical decisions."* Figure 7: chorale 86's surface melody differs from chorale 323's, yet *"both
    chorales are based on the same underlying hymn melody and thus share the same melodic encoding.
    (These sorts of decisions were made on a case-by-case basis.)"*
14. **FACT.** *"To best handle the modal nature of some hymn melodies …, scale degrees were universally
    measured in terms of a parallel major scale, with flats and sharps indicating raised or lowered
    versions (e.g., scale-degree 3 in a minor key was encoded as "b3")."* The encodings are stored in a
    tab-delimited text file; Figure 8 prints an excerpt (§4.3 below). Endnote [8]: the files *"can be
    downloaded from: <http://www.midside.com/publications/chorales/>"*. **This side fetched nothing.**

### §2.5 The cadence encoding (pages 7–8)

15. **FACT (the shorthand).** Three aspects are tracked: *"1) the local key area, 2) the cadence
    classification, and 3) the chordal tone of the soprano at the cadential arrival."* Local key areas
    are Roman numerals, upper case for major and lower case for minor, *"as reconciled with the global
    tonic"* (the paper's example: *"ii in C major is a cadence in the local key of D minor"*). The
    classification is a two-letter abbreviation: perfect authentic (PA), imperfect authentic (IA), half
    (HF), phrygian (PH), plagal (PL), deceptive (DE). The soprano's chord member is an Arabic number.
    The paper's example: *"the notation "III-IA3" in the key of G minor represents an imperfect
    authentic cadence in Bb major with the third of the chord (D) in the soprano at the point of
    cadential arrival."* A slash marked an inverted final chord, *"but this feature was rarely
    needed"*. Figure 9 applies it to chorale 44's first two cadences: I-HF5, V-PA1.
16. **FACT (one added category).** *"This category is what McHose refers to as the "plagal half
    cadence," although I prefer to call it a "subdominant stop" (shorthand, "SS") to avoid confusion
    with other cadence types."* The paper says McHose (1947) discussed it, that it *"has since fallen
    out of common parlance"*, and that it *"is common enough within the chorales to warrant its own
    category"*. Example: chorale 1, bars 13–14 (Figure
    10), where *"the F natural in bar 13, which tonicizes the C-major chord under the fermata, is not
    strong enough to make this arrival sound like an authentic cadence in the key of IV."*
17. **FACT (a residual category).** Some fermatas *"are not preceded by an event that warrants one of
    the cadence categories"*; the example is chorale 65's second fermata after the double bar (Figure
    12), and *"problematic cases like this were categorized simply as "no cadence" ("NC")."*
18. **FACT (the paper's definition of its central word, page 7).** *"In this paper, I will use the term
    "cadence" to mean "the harmonic event at the phrase ending as indicated by the fermata," if only
    because the former is less clumsy."* The paper says readers with Schenkerian leanings (it cites
    Caplin 2004) may not count the subdominant stop, the plagal or the deceptive cadence as cadences,
    and calls the issue *"somewhat moot"* for its purpose; it offers *"fermata events"* as the neutral
    term.
19. **FACT (how the analysis was made, page 8).** *"The actual task of categorizing cadences was done
    by ear at the piano, using the shorthand described above, with the results stored in a separate
    tab-delimited text file (see Figure 11)."* (§4.3 below.)
20. **FACT and CONJECTURE mixed (global key and mode, page 8).** FACT, without a count: *"In some cases,
    the global key of the chorale is not entirely clear. This situation was found to be fairly rare,
    though."* CONJECTURE: *"the global key turns out to be less important than the local key
    implications in terms of what cadence type to expect given a particular melodic pattern."* On modal
    melodies the paper sets Burns (1993, 1994, 1995) against Renwick (1997) and sides with neither; it
    remarks that Bach sets the Phrygian tune of *"Herzlich thut mich verlangen"* in a minor context
    (chorales 21, 89, 270, 286, 345) and a major one (74, 80, 98, 367), and judges the effect of modal
    melodies on the study *"relatively low since the melodic encoding scheme captures any chromatic
    content"*.
21. **FACT (a second analyst, pages 8–9).** Whole, because §5 rests on it: *"One final concern is that
    some cadences are inherently ambiguous. The classic case of tonicization versus modulation comes to
    mind, where it may not be clear, for instance, whether we have a half cadence in the key of tonic
    or an authentic cadence in the key of the dominant. To explore this issue somewhat (i.e., the
    degree to which cadence analysis is a subjective task), I asked another Ph.D. in music theory (David
    Temperley) to analyze a portion of the chorales using the same encoding method described above.
    Within the test subset (about 10% of the total), we had identical encodings for over 95% of the
    cadence events. It was found that our primary differences did, in fact, concern the issue of
    tonicization versus modulation."* And: *"while our agreement level fell short of the 100% ideal, it
    seemed good enough to indicate confidence that music scholars generally classify cadential events
    within the chorales in highly similar ways."*
22. **FACT (size and tooling, page 9).** *"In total, the corpus is encoded with 2,124 unique fermata
    events. The frequency data reported in the following sections were calculated using custom computer
    scripts written in the Python programming language to parse the tab-delimited text files described
    above. Finally, it should be mentioned that all frequency data reported here ignore repeat signs;
    so, for example, chorale 1 is considered to have only six cadences total."*

### §2.6 The specific results the paper leads with (pages 9–10)

23. **FACT.** *"There are 90 unique instances of this 6-6-5 phrase ending (measured in terms of the
    global tonic) within the chorales of Bach."* Endnote [9]: *"none are harmonized with a half cadence
    in tonic."* Table 3 (§4.4 below) gives the distribution; the paper's reading: *"a perfect authentic
    cadence on the dominant is the clear frontrunner"*, and *"the second most common cadence given this
    melodic phrase ending – a plagal cadence in the home key – does not involve scale-degree
    reinterpretation."*
24. **FACT (final cadences).** *"166 of the 177 major-key chorales end with a I-PA1; moreover, 99% end
    with a cadence in the tonic key."* For minor-key chorales, excluding those with Phrygian melodies
    (*"all of which end on a dominant chord in relation to the home key, some via a half or Phrygian
    cadence, others via a modulation to the dominant"*), *"99% of those that end on scale-degree 1 end
    also with a perfect authentic cadence on tonic.[10]"* And: *"in minor-key chorales, final tonic
    cadences with Picardy thirds (I#-PA1) outnumber those without a Picardy third (i-PA1) by a 10-to-1
    ratio."* Endnote [10]: chorale 262 (G minor) ends on the melodic note A over an i-HF5.
25. **FACT.** For the pattern 2-2-1 away from the chorale's end: *"over 97% of the 153 non-final fermata
    events given the melodic pattern 2-2-1 involve a cadence in the tonic key."* Table 4, §4.5 below.
    The paper's conclusion from the two cases: *"Scale-degree reinterpretation thus seems like an
    appropriate technique in certain situations, less appropriate in others."*
26. **FACT (the step from three-note patterns to the last degree alone).** The paper judges a list of
    every three-note ending of little use *"(at least from a pedagogical standpoint)"* and tabulates by
    *"only the final scale degree at the cadential arrival"*, final cadences excluded (Tables 5 and 6,
    §4.6 and §4.7). Its reading of Table 5: *"most cadence types fall into three categories: 1) a
    perfect authentic cadence (PA1); 2) a half cadence with the chordal fifth in the soprano (HF5); and
    3) an imperfect authentic cadence with the chordal third in the soprano (IA3)."* Of Table 6: *"Again,
    PA1, HF5, and IA3 cadences prevail"*, the stated exception being raised scale-degree 7, which takes
    half and Phrygian cadences in the tonic key because *"it is impossible to harmonize a raised
    scale-degree 7 in minor with any cadence in a closely-related (non-tonic) key area."*
27. **FACT (the paper's own summary value, page 11).** *"Overall, the most-common and second-most-common
    cadences account for about 80% of the non-final cadential events in the Bach chorales."* It counts
    *"30 of the cadence types listed in Tables 5 and 6"*. (Arithmetic at §4.10.)

### §2.7 The simplified model (pages 11–12)

28. **CONJECTURE (the model is a proposal; its fit is measured at §2.8).** *"Because PA1, HF5, and IA3
    cadences are so prevalent in the distributions of Tables 5 and 6, I would like to propose a
    simplified model of cadence types and local keys areas."* Tables 7 and 8 (§4.8) map, for each
    soprano scale degree at the arrival, the PA1, HF5 and IA3 cadences available in closely-related
    key areas, *"with key areas organized from left to right by their typicality"*. In words (page 11):
    *"a harmonization default is to interpret the soprano note at the fermata as scale degree 1 (via a
    perfect authentic cadence), 2 (via a half cadence), or 3 (via an imperfect authentic cadence) in
    tonic or some closely-related key area, with the tonic, dominant, and submediant keys being more
    likely destinations (in that order) than the subdominant or supertonic."*
29. **FACT (one measured consequence, page 12).** *"I-HF3 cadences are fairly rare in the major-key
    chorales, accounting for less than 10% of the cadences supporting scale-degree 7. (This finding is
    a notable exception to the list of common cadences that Gauldin proposes shown in Figure 5.)"*
    *(As read, the page prints "Figure 5"; Gauldin's cadences are the paper's Figure 4, and its Figure 5
    is the four chorale openings. See §8.)*
30. **FACT (minor keys, page 12).** *"while Table 6 shows that more cadences overall occur in the tonic
    key, it also shows that there is a tendency to modulate to the relative major whenever possible. For
    this reason, the mediant key (not the minor tonic) is the left-most key area in Table 8."* And:
    *"the subtonic (i.e., the dominant of the relative major) is a more probable key destination than
    the minor dominant."*

### §2.8 How well the model fits, and the four added cases (pages 12–14)

31. **FACT.** *"Its success rate sits at 80.6% overall (1761 internal cadences, 1420 model matches): a
    good result, but not great. (The mismatches between the model and the data are noted with asterisks
    in Tables 7 and 8.)"*
32. **FACT.** *"the success rate of the model is closely linked to the melodic interval leading into the
    cadential arrival. For instance, most melodies descend by step into the cadential arrival (as shown
    in Table 9), and the model fares noticeably better in this situation, with a success rate of roughly
    90%."* Table 9, §4.9. For the ascending third, descending fourth and ascending fourth *"there are
    simply not enough instances in the Bach chorales to make any meaningful estimate of a typical
    solution. (Arguably, the case of unison melodic endings also suffers from too small a sample
    size.)"*
33. **FACT, by the paper's own account reached by trial (page 12).** *"After some trial and error, it
    was found that by adding just four special cases to the core cadences of PA1, HF5, and IA3, we can
    boost the overall success rate well above 90%. These special cases are the deceptive cadence, the
    plagal cadence, the subdominant stop, and a specific contrapuntal cadence."*
34. **FACT (deceptive, page 13).** It *"accounts for 2.5% of cadences in the chorales overall"*.
    *"Post-hoc analysis shows that deceptive cadences seem to be used primarily to add harmonic variety
    to adjacent melodic phrases that end on the same note. In particular, there is a significantly
    higher incidence of deceptive cadences as the penultimate cadence (p < .01; FET).[11]"* Endnote
    [11] names Fisher's exact test and the two proportions compared; **it gives no counts.**
35. **FACT (plagal, page 13).** It *"accounts for 2.8% of cadences overall"*. *"The most common is the
    PL5 type, which typically arises out of melodic upper neighbor motion around scale-degree 5. The
    handful of PL1 cases all arise from a melodic phrase ending that repeats the same note."*
36. **FACT (subdominant stop, page 13).** It *"accounts for 2.7% of all fermata events"*; the two common
    settings *"harmonize a descending melodic third from scale-degree 3 to 1"*; *"subdominant stops are
    especially more probable within the tonic key than in any other key (p < .001; FET).[12]"* Endnote
    [12] likewise gives the comparison and no counts.
37. **FACT and the author's naming (page 14).** *"The final special case I refer to as "expansion to the
    octave." This case involves an ascending melodic line and a descending bass line, each moving by
    step into the final chord. It is worth noting that in every case of expansion to the octave, there
    is half-step motion in one of the outer voices at the cadential arrival."* It covers *"certain
    classes of imperfect authentic, half, and phrygian cadences"* (Figure 16). The opposite, contraction
    to an octave, *"is not a common feature in the Bach chorales. The exception is the "inverted"
    Phrygian cadence …, which ends many minor-key chorales."*
38. **FACT (the flowchart's fit, page 15).** *"Figure 18 organizes the trends described above into a
    flowchart of cadence choices that achieves a success rate of 92.2% in accounting for cadence choices
    in the chorales."* In words: *"it suggests that the final note in the melody be interpreted as
    scale-degree 1, 2, or 3 in tonic or some closely-related key area, unless the final note is raised
    scale-degree 7 in minor (thus it must be a half cadence in tonic), part of an upper neighbor motion
    around scale-degree 5 (consider a plagal cadence in tonic), or ascended to by a whole step (consider
    a Phrygian cadence in tonic or some closely-related key)."* **No counts behind the 92.2% are given
    on the pages as read.** The chart itself is set down at §4.11.

### §2.9 The discussion and the paper's own stated limits (pages 15–17)

39. **FACT, loosely stated.** *"a relatively small number of scenarios account for the vast majority of
    the 2,000+ cadences in the chorale harmonizations of Bach."*
40. **FACT (against Salzer and Schachter, page 16).** Their sample harmonization of *"Freu' dich sehr, o
    meine Seele"* (Figure 20; 1969/1989, p. 294) ends both opening phrases in the tonic key, and they
    call the first phrase *"unsatisfactory"* in context for want of variety. The paper's counter:
    *"over a third of the chorales include consecutive tonic cadences. (Chorales 86 and 323 have five
    authentic cadences in tonic in a row!)"* Bach's own setting (chorale 64, Figure 21) takes the first
    phrase to *"a perfect authentic cadence in the dominant"*. The paper's argument is that their first
    cadence is unstylistic *"because of how it handles the melodic structure"*, not because two tonic
    cadences follow each other. *(As read, Figure 21's caption prints the hymn's first word as "Frue'",
    where the text and Figures 19 and 20 print "Freu'".)*
41. **The paper's stated limits (pages 16–17).**
    - *"this study has ignored other important factors in Bach's compositional process"* — the hymn
      texts are named (Pirro 1907/2010) — and *"the structure of the melody goes only so far to explain
      every cadential choice"*.
    - *"It was assumed in this study that the hymn tunes were pre-compositional entities, i.e., that
      Bach's modulation and cadence choices were tailored to fit the melody. But the actual scenario (at
      least in some cases) may be the reverse."*
    - Cadence choice is a *"small slice of the harmonization process"*; inner-voice leading and bass
      patterns are left to *"a future study"*, which the paper says *"would add a more objective element
      to the somewhat subjective task of cadence classification"* and *"may even suggest new or
      overlapping cadence categories beyond those considered here."*
    - On teaching by paradigm, the paper quotes Salzer and Schachter's warning against restricting a
      student *"to the most typical, frequently encountered usages"* (1969/1989, p. 305) and answers
