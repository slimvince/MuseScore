## §4 — The formalism, as the paper states it

### §4.1 The two principles (printed 37–38)

The paper motivates the grammar from one example, *"C A⁷ Dm G C"*, printed as (a), and nine alterations
of it lettered (b) to (j) (37). *(★ CORRECTED AT THE CROSS-CHECK, §9.3. FORMER WORDING, PRESERVED (#12):
"and ten variants of it lettered (a) to (j)" — (a) is the base sequence itself; the page says "The
first set of alterations of the base sequence (a)".)* Four are starred as unacceptable: (c) *C A⁷ G C*,
(d) *C A⁷ Dm C*, (i) *C A⁷ C G C* and (j) *C A⁷ E♭m G C*. Unstarred: (b) *C Dm G C*, (e) *C G C*, and
three replacements of *Dm* — (f) *D⁷*, (g) *A♭⁷*, which the text allows *"In a Jazz context"*, and (h)
*F♯⁰*, which it says *"would be possible if the leading note in A⁷ is continued downwards"*. *(★ The two
conditions were added at the cross-check, when printed 37 was requested again; this file first listed
(g) and (h) as acceptable without them.)* From the deletions the paper concludes that *A⁷* depends on *Dm* and not on the
preceding *C*, and that *"Dm and G, grouped with their dependent antecedent chords A⁷ and A⁷ Dm, are
both dependent on their consequent chord as whole blocks"* (37). **The acceptability marks are the
author's judgments; no source or listener study is cited for them on that page.**

**The dependency principle** (38): *"each element (chord) in a chord sequence is structurally connected
to its preceding or succeeding chord or chord group in a dependency relationship. Each group of
dependent chords (which may contain more than two elements) recursively distinguishes a head on which
the other elements of that group are dependent."* And: *"The fact that dependency relationships to
non-adjacent groups are disallowed forces the dependency structure to constitute a planar tree."* Two
consequences the paper draws: long-distance dependencies between chords separated by others (*C* and
*G* in the example); and adjacent chords that are structurally unrelated because they sit in different
branches (*C* to *A⁷*). One empirical citation is attached: Woolhouse and Rohrmeier [35] *"found that
chord progressions without the same parent node exhibit smaller values of chord attraction than
structurally connected surface chords"* — reference [35] is a conference talk (52).

**Functional heads** (38): *"chords are organized into functional categories which describe their tonal
function which may be instantiated or modified by different chords. Following a functional approach
[2], the three main tonal functions are tonic, dominant and predominant."* Dominants *"would include
chords such as V, VII or ♭II (in the case of Jazz harmony)"*.

### §4.2 The symbol sets (printed 39)

*"The formalism distinguishes four levels: a phrase level, a functional level, a scale degree level,
and the surface level."* Its sets, as printed: phrase-level symbols ℙ = {piece, P}; key symbols
𝕂 = {Cmaj, Cmin, C♯maj, C♯min, D♭maj, D♭min, …}; functional region symbols ℝ = {TR, SR, DR}; functional
terms 𝔽 = {t, s, d, tp, sp, dp, tcp}; scale degrees and relative scale degrees
𝕊 = {I, II, …, VII, V/I, V/II, …, VII/I, VII/II, …}; surface chord symbols 𝕆 (§3 above). *"The
generation begins on the phrase or the functional level and continues recursively until a sequence of
surface symbols is generated (or parsed)."*

### §4.3 Phrase level — rules (1) to (3) (printed 39)

| Rule | As printed |
|---|---|
| (1) | piece_{key = x ∈ 𝕂} → P⁺ |
| (2) | P → TR |
| (3) | piece → TR_{key = x ∈ 𝕂} |

*"A phrase ending on a perfect cadence is created by rule 2 through the generation of a single tonic
seed which will be recursively expanded to a full sequence through subsequent rules on the functional
level"* *(★ corrected at §10; it read "A phrase ending on a perfect cadence comes from rule (2)
alone")*; *"A phrase ending on V or a half
cadence is generated by rules 2 and 6"*; *"a singleton dominant seed (without a preceding or subsequent
tonic reference) cannot be generated by this grammar"*; *"The plagal cadence is created based on rules
2 and 21."* Rules (1) and (2) are kept to fit phrase-based forms *"as in Jazz or in pop music"*; for

**Key inheritance.** *"All functional rules pass on their key property assigned from the superordinate
parent nodes to their children."* (40) Where a rule does not write the key, the symbol passes it on
unchanged; the paper's example is *"TP_{key=y} → DP_{key=y} TP_{key=y}"* (41) — printed with the symbols
*TP* and *DP*, which are not members of any set defined at printed 39 (see §7).

**Rule (15), in the paper's words** (41): *"each functional region may itself become a local tonic
during the generation/derivation process. For instance, a d, tp or s node may become the new local
tonic dominating all the respective children of that node."* And: *"X, representing any functional term
except the tonic, may be rewritten as the new (local) tonic which defines the new key according to the
respective function and scale degree of X. This constitutes the only way in which a functional term (in
𝔽) can reenter the recursive domain of functional regions (in ℝ)."* The function ψ(f, k) turns a
functional term and a reference key into a key: *"ψ(d, B♭ maj) = F maj, ψ(tp, A♭ maj) = F min"*. A
restriction: *"the (dominant) diminished VII chord in major cannot instantiate a modulation, since it
does not define a valid mode property."* Endnote 4 (51): *"in the case of a modulation in minor, the
definition of ψ lets sp refer to VI and not ♭II, e.g. ψ(sp, Amin) = Fmaj."* **ψ is described by these
examples and by *"the common definition of functional terms within the diatonic framework"*; no table
or closed definition of ψ was met in the pages as read.**

**Pivot chords** (41): *"the double generation of the pivot chord from two different branches of the
parse tree constitutes the preferred form of analysis that captures the double role of the pivot
chord"*. At printed 45 the paper names the alternative — generating the pivot from both adjacent
branches — and says it *"would result in the loss of the mathematical tree structure that requires the
branches to be disjunct."* So one surface chord stands under two leaves (the *"="* signs of Figure 3).

**No line is drawn between modulation and tonicization** *(★ corrected at §10; the heading read
"Modulation and tonicization are one thing in this grammar")* (42): *"The grammar incorporates no
distinction between modulations, brief tonicizations or changes of local diatonic context. This entails
that the difference between these phenomena is gradual and that the stability of a (change of) key is
greater, the higher the node is located in the tree and the more children it dominates."*

### §4.5 Scale degree level — rules (17) to (27), and typing (printed 42–43)

| Rule | As printed |
|---|---|
| (17) | X → D(X) X, for any X ∈ 𝕊 |
| (18) | X → Δ(X) X, for any X ∈ 𝕊 |
| (19) | D(X) → V/VI/X \| VII/VI/X, if X refers to a diminished triad; V/X \| VII/X, otherwise |
| (20) | t → I |
| (21) | t → I IV I |
| (22) | s → IV |
| (23) | d → V \| VII |
| (24) | tp → VI if key is major; III if key is minor |
| (25) | dp → VII if key is minor |
| (26) | sp → II if key is major; VI, ♭II if key is minor |
| (27) | tcp → III if key is major; VI if key is minor |

**Rule (17)** lets a chord be preceded by its secondary dominants, *"which may be outside the
surrounding diatonic context"*; **rule (18)** gives diatonic descending-fifth sequences. D(X) *"assigns
the scale degree of a perfect fifth above X"* and *"assumes a missing fundamental with respect to the
diminished triads (for instance, F♯⁰ would be treated like a D major (seventh) chord)"*; Δ(X) *"assigns
the scale degree of a fifth above X within the diatonic scale (modulo 7)"* (42). The paper's examples
for rule (18) are *"Autumn leaves"* (*Cm F⁷ B♭maj7 E♭maj7 Am^ø7 D⁷ Gm*) and *"Fly me to the moon"*
(*Cm Fm⁷ B♭⁷ E♭maj7 A♭maj7 D^ø7 G⁷ Cm*).

**Why secondary dominants are not done by modulation** (42): in a chain such as *A⁷ D⁷ G*, *"the first
dominant could not be explained by modulation and a d t progression, since the chord D⁷ cannot fulfil a
double function as relative tonic and a dominant seventh chord at the same time within this
formalism."* And both rules sit on the scale degree level *"in order to avoid the reentry of any part
of the sequence into the whole recursive generative process"*. Endnote 7 (51) says rules (17) and (18)
*"create ambiguities with the cadential rules 4 and 5"* and defends both sets as *"conceptually
different and meaningful parts of the grammar"*.

**On the interface rules** (43): the Neapolitan chord *"is subsumed under the subdominant parallels
sp"*, which the paper calls *"more economic (saving two rules)"*; III is left out of dp in major
because *"in common-practice or Jazz harmony, III cannot typically replace V"*; *"The functional
reading of III as dp which relates to VI as tp is reflected in a generalized way in rules (17) and
(18)"*; one use of III
progressing to IV *"is indirectly modelled by rule 12"*, and endnote 8 offers a scale-degree rule
*IV → III IV* as the alternative to rule (12).

**Why the two levels are decoupled** (43): *"First, deep structural relationships for chord sequences
that differ on the scale degree level are maintained, and, secondly, different structural
implementations within a functional framework such as [39] become possible."*

**Voice-leading rules are named, with one instance given** *(★ corrected at §10; it read "named and not
given")* (43): *"a number of voice-leading rules may be
postulated"*, with one instance, the six-four suspension over V written as a rewrite of V. Altered
chords *"would be located on this level"*. *"The description of the specific details of specialized and
stylistic rules will be a matter of fine-grained style-specific rules that do not belong to the general
formalism presented here."*

**Typing** (3.3.3, printed 43): when a chord is derived through several functions by modulation, *"the
last function in the chain of derivation defines its surface function and form. For instance, a tonic
chord which acts as a higher order dominant will have the surface of a tonic and not a dominant
seventh."* Surface features that mark a function — a major triad with a minor seventh, a
half-diminished seventh chord, a major chord with an added sixth — *"may be expressed in rules like
d → V⁷ or s → IV⁶"*; *"Such constraints are very effective for disambiguation during the parsing
process"*, and they *"are not discussed in detail here and have to be addressed in future work when
modelling a specific style [34]."*

### §4.6 Surface level — rule (28) (printed 44)

Scale degrees become chords given the key, *"for instance: V⁷_{key=E♭ maj} → B♭⁷"*; the paper calls these
rules *"trivial"* and does not number them. **Rule (28): X → X⁺, for any X ∈ 𝕆** — a surface chord may
repeat. It sits below the functional rules because *"a mere repetition of chords without change of
function or scale degree is regarded as a phenomenon located at a surface level which does not enter
recursive expansion"*.

## §6 — The claims, labeled

Labels as this file uses them: **FACT** — the pages state or show it as something the paper does or
contains; **THEORY** — a claim the paper argues from cited theory or from its own examples;
**CONJECTURE** — a claim the paper itself hedges, or makes with no support met on the pages.

1. **FACT.** The paper gives a context-free rule set in four levels — 28 numbered rules, rule (3) an
   alternative to (1) and (2) — over chord-symbol sequences with a key property passed down the tree
   (§4.2–§4.6).
2. **FACT.** Modulation is one rule, (15): a functional term becomes the tonic region of a new key given
   by ψ. Change of mode is a second rule, (16). The grammar *"incorporates no distinction"* between
   modulation and tonicization; the paper calls the difference gradual and ties a key's stability to
   the height of its node and how much it dominates (§4.4).
3. **FACT.** Secondary dominants and diatonic fifth chains are scale-degree-level rules, (17) and (18),
   placed there so that they do not re-enter the functional recursion (§4.5).
4. **FACT.** The grammar is ambiguous, and the paper says so; this reader met no probabilities and no
   preference rules in it; it names typing constraints as a means of disambiguation without giving them in
   detail, and names meter, phrase length and preference rules as needed for an implementation (§4.7).
5. **FACT.** The input is chord symbols without inversion or figured bass; the key of the piece is
   given; bass motion is said to need independent modeling (§3).
6. **FACT.** No measurement was met in the pages as read. The paper's support for its rules is one constructed example with
   acceptability marks and four hand-drawn analyses, one of which (Figure 6, Analysis 2) it presents as
   a difficulty for the grammar (§5).
7. **THEORY.** Harmonic sequences are organized by recursive dependency with functional heads — tonic,
   dominant, predominant — after the Riemannian tradition (cited as [2,37,38] at printed 39 and as
   [2,41] at printed 48) and Kostka and Payne [33] (§4.1). The paper adds that the sufficiency of its
   compact rule set for its examples *"accords with the statements given in [33,42,43] that tonal
   harmony is fundamentally grounded in elaborations of cadential harmony"* (48) *(★ corrected at §10;
   it read "The paper adds that this accords with statements in [33,42,43]", which left unclear what
   accords)*.
8. **THEORY.** The paper argues that Markov and n-gram models cannot express these dependencies
   (49–50): they *"offer no way
   of deriving harmonic sequences from primitives"*; *"a Markovian model of chords that is rich enough
   to model modulation possesses no comparable structural memory and will generate a 'Brownian'
   harmonic motion that will only randomly (if at all) return to its original or previous key"*; and

9. **THEORY.** Third progressions: some are licensed by functional substitution (*IV–II*, *V–VII*) or

13. **CONJECTURE.** That tree matching on such parses models harmonic similarity — *"bears potential"*
