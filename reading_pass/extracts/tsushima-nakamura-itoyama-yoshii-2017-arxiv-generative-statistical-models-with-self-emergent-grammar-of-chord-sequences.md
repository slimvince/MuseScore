## 2. The input and the repertoire, established at page 1 and at the paper's own data section (e⁸)

**The input is CHORD SYMBOLS** — established at page 1 by (c⁸) and again here at §3 and §5.1. §3
defines a sequence as *"x = x_{1:N} = (x_1, …, x_N), where each x_n takes values in the set of chord
symbols (output symbol space) Ω"*; §1 says in terms *"we use chord-sequence data in the popular/jazz
notation"* and that *"the method is independent of particular music styles or systems of chord
notation and is applicable to other chord notations such as the Roman numeral notation and the
figured bass notation."* **No notes, no score and no audio enter any model the paper runs**; §5.5's Figure 13 sketches,
without running it, a chord model joined to a feature model for recognition.

**Two datasets, §5.1 (page 8), and PCFG models were run on only one of them:**

- **J-pop:** chord sequences of **3500 J-pop songs** from a public web page (footnote 3: J-Total
  Music). *"All songs had key information in the major mode and were transposed to C major key. Each
  sequence in this dataset corresponds to a whole piece and the average length was 119.5 (chords)."*
  **"The computation time required for learning and inferring PCFG models using such long sequences
  is practically too large and only Markov models and HMMs were tested using this data."**
- **Billboard:** sequences extracted from the McGill **Billboard dataset** (reference [48], Burgoyne,
  Wild & Fujinaga 2011), sampled from the Billboard Hot 100 of 1958–1991. *"Using the musical
  structure annotation, 1702 chord sequences with a length larger than 7 were obtained by segmenting
  468 popular pieces in units of sections such as verse and chorus."* Tonic transposed to C; because
  the Billboard data has tonic but not mode, *"the obtained sequences included C minor sequences as
  well as more frequent C major sequences."* Average length **13.0**. **"We tested all types of models
  using this dataset."**
- **Splits:** test data 500 J-pop sequences and 171 Billboard sequences (one tenth); training
  subsets of 30, 300 and 3000 (J-pop) and 30, 300 and 1531 (Billboard) sequences.
- **Symbol spaces:** the top 10, 20 or 50 chord symbols per dataset with every other symbol mapped to
  *Other*, giving N_Ω = 10+1, 20+1, 50+1. Tables 2 and 3 (pages 8–9) list the symbols and the
  cumulative proportions: J-pop 79.75 % / 90.92 % / 98.20 %, Billboard 58.42 % / 69.55 % / 84.89 %.

**So the repertoire is popular music — Japanese pop and Anglo-American chart pop — as chord symbols,
with the tonality removed as a variable by transposition to C.** That last clause matters for L2:
the paper's models answer *what chord follows what* inside a fixed tonic, and the tonality question
the L2 charter entangles with it (`FRAMEWORK.md` L2, *"what is the tonality at each moment"*) is not
present in their input at all.

**The paper holds NO harmonic ground truth.** No Roman numeral, no functional label and no analyst's
reading is annotated on either dataset. The words *tonic*, *dominant* and *subdominant* appear in §5.4.2
as the authors' own interpretation of learned categories — Figure 11's caption says *"States are
manually sorted for clearer illustration"* — and nowhere as a target the models are graded against.
The *"ground-truth symbol"* of §4.2 (equation 31) is the **next chord symbol in the data**, not an
analysis label.

## 3. What the paper actually does — the method, stated in the paper's own structure

**§3.1 Markov models.** First- to k-th-order Markov models over chord symbols, maximum-likelihood
counts, with three smoothings tested (§5.1): additive (constant 0.1), Kneser–Ney (KN) and modified
Kneser–Ney (MKN), *"which incrementally use lower-order transition probabilities to obtain higher-order
transition probabilities"*, the choice grounded in reference [31] (Chen & Goodman 1999) as *"the most
effective smoothing method"* for language modelling.

**§3.2 Self-emergent HMMs.** A latent state z_n per chord symbol, first-order Markov over states,
categorical output per state; *"We interpret the latent states as chord categories."* Learned by EM
(Baum–Welch) and, in the Bayesian extension, by Gibbs sampling (GS) with Dirichlet priors (parameter
0.1), the maximum-likelihood sample then polished by EM. State-space sizes tested: 1–9, 10, 15, 20,
25, 30, 40, …, 90, 100 (equation 40). *Self-emergent* is the authors' own term (§1): *"models whose
latent variables and grammar are completely inferred through unsupervised learning using data without
annotations."*

**§3.3 Self-emergent PCFG models.** Binary production rules S → z_L z_R, z → z_L z_R, z → x (with
S → x set to zero since no sequence has length one); nonterminals interpreted as chord categories;
EM (inside-outside) and GS (inside-filtering outside-sampling); nonterminal counts N_Δ = 1–9, 10, 15,
20. A small constant η = 0.01/N_Δ is added to the HMM-derived rule probabilities (§4.1, equation 25)
so that PCFG parameters initialised from an HMM can move away from the linear-chain constraint
z = z_L, which *"cannot be relaxed during the learning process … once imposed"*.

**§4.1 Relations among the models.** Smaller HMMs and PCFG models can be mimicked by larger ones;
HMMs include Markov models of any order; PCFG models include HMMs — the inclusion *"strictly true only
when HMMs and PCFG models are appropriately reformulated as we show in Appendix A"*, the strict form
needing **twice as many nonterminals** as the HMM has states. The approximate embedding (equations
23–26) is used to **initialise PCFG parameters from a learned HMM**, which §5.3 then tests as an
alternative to random initialisation.

**§4.2 Evaluation measures.** Test-data **perplexity** (equation 29, the exponential of the
cross-entropy over the whole test set, with the PCFG evidence probability normalised per sequence
length as Appendix B derives); the **symbol-wise predictive error rate** (equation 31 — predict each
symbol from all the others in its sequence and count misses); and the **reciprocal of the mean
reciprocal rank** (equation 32). §5.2.1 (page 14, Figure 6) reports that perplexity and error rate are
*"highly correlated especially for lower values"*, and that RMRR tracks the error rate closely.

**§4.3 Information-theoretical analysis.** Four quantities of a learned HMM — the perplexity of the
stationary state distribution P_Γ (effective state-space size), the average output perplexity P_φ
(how many symbols a state emits), the average state-association variety V (how many states a symbol
is emitted from), and the average transition perplexity P_π — defined to read how a learned HMM uses
its states.

## 4. The record's three joined claims, each answered separately at the object (e⁸)

The record's *against* sentence, quoted whole from `FRAMEWORK.md` **line 794–797** (DP-O, read at
the object this sitting): *"Against it: tree models are harder to optimise and *often perform no
better* than latent-category sequence models of matched size, and induced categories correspond to
textbook harmonic functions only while the models are small. [FACT — both.]"* And from **line
1865–1868** (§S6's DP15, read at the object this sitting): *"Against it, Tsushima and colleagues find
that their tree models are harder to optimise and *often perform no better* than latent-category
sequence models of matched size, and that induced categories correspond to textbook harmonic
functions only while the models are small. [FACT.]"*

**Claim 1 — "tree models are harder to optimise." SUPPORTED; part MEASURED, part ASSERTED, and the
paper's own word is *difficulty* rather than *harder*.**
- *Measured:* §5.3 (page 15), on the Billboard data at N_Ω = 20+1, Figures 8 and 9: *"For N_X = 300
  and 1531, the PCFG models worked at most equivalently and often worse than the HMMs with N_Γ = N_Δ.
  There is a clear tendency that the HMM initialisations yielded better results than the random
  initialisations in the range 4 ≤ N_Δ ≤ 10. These results show the difficulty of parameter
  optimisation for PCFG models and the effectiveness of the HMM initialisation in some cases."* And,
  closing §5.3 (page 16): *"These results again confirm that there are many local optima for the
  PCFG-model parameters and it is difficult to find good optima by random initialisation even with the
  GS algorithm."* Figure 9 shows the spread of test-data perplexity across initialisations per
  PCFG size.
- *Asserted:* §4.1 (page 6), citing reference [39]: *"It has been reported that the initialisation
  problem is more severe for PCFG models than HMMs because of the complex model structure and the
  existence of many local optima."* And §5.5 (page 18): *"the PCFG models require time complexity of
  square order with respect to the sequence length and cubic order with respect to the number of
  nonterminals, which is typically much larger than that of HMMs"* — a complexity statement, not a
  timing measurement — with §5.1's consequence that the PCFG models **could not be run on the J-pop
  data at all** for cost.
- *Bound:* the measured half is on one dataset (Billboard), at one symbol space shown (N_Ω = 20+1,
  §5.3 saying *"the results for other cases were similar"*), with **30 random seeds for N_Δ ≤ 6 and 4
  to 6 seeds for N_Δ ≥ 7** because of computation time.

**Claim 2 — "often perform no better than latent-category sequence models of matched size."
SUPPORTED at the object and MEASURED — but *matched size* means matched STATE COUNT, which is not
matched capacity, and the axis is predictive power.**
- *Measured:* §5.3 (page 15): *"For N_X = 300 and 1531, the PCFG models worked at most equivalently
  and often worse than the HMMs with N_Γ = N_Δ"* (test-data perplexity); *"For error rates, we again
  find that overall the PCFG models worked at most equivalently and often worse than the HMMs with
  N_Γ = N_Δ."* §5.5 (page 18): *"Given the result that PCFG models did not much improve the
  predictive power of chord sequences compared to HMMs, there would be little interest in using PCFG
  models for simple recognition tasks."* Two qualifications the paper states beside it: *"for N_X = 30
  and 300, the lowest test-data perplexity for the PCFG models was less than that of the Markov
  models"*, and *"The lowest error rate for the PCFG models was less than that of the Markov models
  for N_X = 30 and they were even for N_X = 300."*
- *What "matched size" is at the object:* the comparison sets **N_Γ = N_Δ** — the same number of
  latent states as nonterminals (Figure 8's horizontal axis is *"Model size N_Γ/N_Δ"*). **It is not
  matched parameter count.** Table 1 (page 4) gives the free-parameter counts: HMM
  (1+N_Γ)(N_Γ−1)+N_Γ(N_Ω−1); PCFG (1+N_Δ)(N_Δ²−1)+N_Δ N_Ω. At equal N the PCFG carries more
  parameters, the difference growing with N. **So the record's phrase *matched size* is the paper's
  own matching and is right as far as it goes; the record's falsifier two lines below says
  *matched-capacity*, and the paper's comparison is not that.** See §7, finding (3).
- *The axis:* **predictive power over the next chord symbol** — perplexity and symbol-wise error rate
  on held-out chord sequences. No harmonic ground truth is involved (§2 above). The falsifier's axis
  is *"this repertoire's ground truth"*. See §7, finding (5).
- *Bound:* Billboard data only; PCFG models were not run on J-pop.

**Claim 3 — "induced categories correspond to textbook harmonic functions only while the models are
small." PARTLY SUPPORTED, ASSERTED by inspection rather than measured — and the word *only* is the
record's, not the paper's.**
- *What the paper says:* Abstract: *"the self-emergent categories often correspond to traditional
  harmonic functions."* §1 (page 2): *"the learned categories often correspond to traditional
  harmonic functions when the model size is small, which confirms the results of unsupervised chord
  categorisation [42, 43]."* §6: *"the learned categories often corresponded to traditional harmonic
  functions when the model size is small."* §5.4.2 (page 17), on Figure 11 — an HMM with N_Γ = 4 on
  J-pop and a PCFG with N_Δ = 5 on Billboard: *"The first three states/nonterminals in these examples
  can be identified as the tonic, subdominant, and dominant functions"*; *"Depending on initial
  parameters, for both HMMs and PCFG models, the learned categories were not always related to
  traditional harmonic functions as clearly as the above examples."*
- *How it is established:* **by the authors' inspection of two hand-sorted examples** (Figure 11,
  *"States are manually sorted"*) and one transition diagram (Figure 12, the four-state HMM read as
  *"a probabilistic representation of the familiar cadence structure"*). **No quantitative measure of
  correspondence to harmonic functions is defined or reported anywhere in the paper.** The
  information-theoretical measures of §5.4.1 describe how states are used, not whether they match a
  function label.
- *What the paper does NOT say:* it does not report that large models' categories **fail** to
  correspond. What §5.4.1 reports of larger HMMs (Figure 10, N_Γ up to 100) is that *"the same chord
  symbols are represented by multiple latent states depending on their context, which has a similar
  effect as symbol refinement for natural language models"* — a different observation. The paper's
  own qualifier on non-correspondence is **initialisation**, not size: *"Depending on initial
  parameters … not always related … as clearly."*
- *Two wording differences:* the record writes **textbook**; the paper writes **traditional**
  throughout. The record writes **only while small**, an exclusive claim; the paper writes **often
  … when small**, a frequency claim with no statement about large models. **The exclusive form is
  not in the paper.** See §7, finding (2).

**The `[FACT — both]` tag, read against this.** Claims 1 and 2 are stated and measured in the paper
(with the size/capacity and axis bounds above). Claim 3 is stated in the paper in a weaker form
than the record carries. **So the tag is right that all three are the paper's statements; it does
not by itself distinguish a measured result from an inspected example, and for claim 3 the record's
sentence is wider than the paper's.**

## 5. What the paper reports, and what each figure is a figure OF

**The paper prints almost no numerical result in its text.** Its numerical results are Figures 2–10 —
curves of test-data perplexity, error rate and the information measures against parameter count or
model size, for two datasets, three symbol spaces and three training sizes, over Markov models under

labels in the held rendering**, so no perplexity or error-rate
value can be transcribed from them. What is printed as a number:

- Tables 2 and 3: the most frequent chord symbols per dataset and the cumulative proportions quoted
  at §2 above.
- The dataset figures quoted at §2 (3500; 119.5; 1702; 468; 13.0; 500; 171; 30/300/3000; 30/300/1531).
- Learning constants: additive smoothing 0.1; Dirichlet parameter 0.1; η = 0.01/N_Δ; κ = 0.5416
  from equation 28 on the data; at most 500 (HMM) or 200 (PCFG) EM iterations, 500 (200) GS
  samplings, 50 polishing EM iterations, convergence at relative likelihood difference 10⁻⁵; 30
  random seeds for N_Γ ≤ 20 and 10 otherwise; PCFG seeds as at §4.
- Qualitative results in words: *"for all N_X and for all N_Ω, the lowest test-data perplexity by the
  HMMs was less than the lowest value by the Markov models"* on the J-pop data; overfitting for N_X = 30 and 300 at
  some N_Γ between 1 and 100; for N_X = 3000 both quantities still decreasing at N_Γ = 100; on Billboard at
  N_X = 1531 and N_Ω = 20+1 and 50+1 the third-order Markov models *"less than or similar to the best
  results for the HMMs"*; *"If the perplexities or error rates for models with similar numbers of
  parameters are compared, HMMs consistently outperformed Markov models"*; GS better than EM for
  N_X = 1531 and N_Δ ≥ 50 (this last figure is as printed on page 9, though N_Δ elsewhere reaches
  20 — recorded as printed, not reconciled); §5.2.2's conclusion that *"training data of size N_X ≥
  300 were able to accurately predict the optimal model for unseen data for model sizes without
  overfitting"*; §5.3's PCFG-against-HMM
  result at §4 above; §5.4.1's P_Γ rising *"roughly proportionally to the model size"*, P_φ falling
  with size but *"larger than 2 even for N_Γ = 100"*, V rising, P_π rising slowly.

**Every figure is a figure of predictive power over chord-symbol sequences in pop repertoire, or of
a learned model's internal structure. None is an accuracy against a human harmonic analysis, none is
a tree accuracy, and none is on this project's repertoire.** The record cites this paper by
