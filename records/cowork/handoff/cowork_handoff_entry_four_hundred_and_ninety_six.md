1. **The paper says its similarity measure may be replaced [checked, p. 291, §3.1.4, left column, end of the second
   paragraph].** In substance: the similarity between melodies used here can be substituted by other methods, such as
   those described in its reference 21 — Hewlett, W.B. (Ed.), *Melodic Similarity: Concepts, Procedures, and
   Application*, Computing in Musicology Vol. 11, The MIT Press, 1998 (pp. 298–299, as printed). The same paragraph
   gives the reason a measure was defined at all: GTTM says nothing in particular about the similarity between two
   written melodies, so the authors newly define a simple one to compute D6(i). Two passages bear on it:
   p. 286, §2.1.1 — GTTM gives no detailed definition of parallelism (counted as ambiguity of the theory itself),
   and that various definitions of parallelism are conceivable is counted as ambiguity of interpretation;
   p. 287, §2.2.1 — various definitions of melodic parallelism are possible (reference 21).
2. **The method is limited to one melodic line without chords [checked, pp. 287–288].** GTTM treats every piece as
   homophony; this study restricts its analysis to monophony. The footnote on p. 288 defines homophony as a single
   melody including chords and monophony as a single melody without them. The evaluation data (p. 293, §4.1) is 100
   melodies of 8 bars cut from classical pieces.
3. **A detail not in the earlier entries [checked, p. 291, right column]:** exGTTM assumes that note sequences shifted
   by less than a set length have no parallelism, and sets that length to one quarter-note beat. The formulas of
   eq. 15 to 17 and the bound "half of L" (p. 292) agree with entry 495 §2 item 2.
