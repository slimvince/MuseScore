# The decision groups — a worked preview

> **What this document is for.** The decision harvest collected every statement in
> the repository that reads like a ruling — 15,224 of them. This step proposes which of
> those statements are restatements of **one** decision, so that the session that
> writes the decisions register reads groups rather than 15,224 separate lines.
>
> Below are the 5 largest groups, each written up in the shape a decisions-register
> entry would take. **Please read them and say whether that shape is readable** —
> before the expensive session commits to it. There is also a finding at the end
> that changes what this grouping is worth; it is short and it matters more than the
> examples do.
>
> **Nothing was decided here, and nothing was thrown away.** This step does not judge
> whether a statement really is a decision, whether a decision still stands, or
> whether the code obeys it. And all 15,224 statements are still present and still
> individually readable in `decision_candidates.json` — this is a grouping laid on
> top, in which every group names every statement it contains.

## How two statements were judged to be the same decision

Only by **wording**. Each statement is cut into overlapping runs of three
consecutive words, and two statements are proposed as the same decision when they
share at least **80%** of those runs — four fifths of the three-word runs in common,
which is restatement in near-identical words rather than merely writing about the
same subject. Capitalisation, punctuation and Markdown formatting are ignored;
numbers are not, because a number is content. Statements shorter than 8 words are
grouped only when their wording is *exactly* the same, since a short heading shares
three-word runs with unrelated short headings.

Two further signals were computed but deliberately **not** allowed to group anything:
statements citing the same commit or document, and statements carrying the same date
and the same person ratifying. Both are recorded as links *between* groups, because
either one on its own would join statements that merely share a context. The rule
throughout was: when in doubt, do not group — a wrong join disappears from view
afterwards, whereas a group that is too coarse is merely untidy and can be split.

Where the wording was strong enough to group but the members do not appear to say
the same thing — different numbers in otherwise identical sentences, or one member
carrying a word like *superseded* that the others lack — the group is **flagged and
left alone**. Those flags are where a change of mind is most likely hiding.

## The finding — please read this

**Grouping by wording barely reduces the work, and the reason is that this
repository does not restate decisions by copying them. It restates them in fresh
words every time.**

Across all 15,224 collected statements there are only **93 pairs** anywhere in the
repository that are near-identical in wording at all — and that count is exact, not
an estimate: it was checked by comparing every possible pair by brute force. Almost
all of those pairs are repeated layout or repeated dispatch instructions. So the
15,224 statements fall into 14,460 groups: the grouping merges 764 statements and leaves
14,274 standing alone.

Those say the same thing and share almost no wording, so no measurement of wording

which is very nearly the number of statements.

### What this does and does not settle

What the grouping does deliver: the repeated layout and repeated instruction text is
now identified and set aside as a labelled bucket (74 groups, 373 occurrences), and
the handful of genuine copy-and-paste restatements is found with certainty. What it
does not deliver is the consolidation the exercise was aiming at, and no adjustment
of the wording cut-off would change that. Loosening it from 0.80 all the way to 0.70
merges only 41 further statements; tightening it to 0.95 un-merges 38. The whole
range from 0.70 to 0.95 spans 79 groups out of 14,460.

This is flagged rather than worked around, because working around it would mean
changing what a "statement" is — cutting the collected blocks into single sentences,
or matching by meaning rather than by wording. Either is a real change of method with
its own risks, and neither is what this step was asked to do.

The set-aside groups are of two kinds, and each can be confirmed a kind at a time
rather than one statement at a time:

- **repeated standing instructions — the "push to our own copy only", "stop if surprised" and "read-only" lines every dispatch repeats**: 50 groups, 159 occurrences.
- **repeated layout — section titles and table header rows**: 24 groups, 214 occurrences.

Two things were deliberately **not** set aside, because setting something aside is
itself a form of grouping and the same when-in-doubt rule applies: a whole line in
bold is not treated as a mere heading (in this repository such a line often carries a
rule — the open-items sections above are the case in point), and the open-items
banner is not treated as an instruction, because it states a rule rather than
directing a session. Both therefore appear above as ordinary groups, where they are
visible.
